# Case Type: Social Impact

Social impact cases involve recommending interventions, programs, or strategic shifts for NGOs, foundations, social ventures, policy bodies addressing social problems, or development organizations.

**Calibration note:** Social impact patterns described here are extrapolated from the meta-playbook + standard impact-measurement frameworks (effective altruism research, RCT literature, theory-of-change methodology), not directly observed from a social impact corpus. Treat as plausible starting points; adjust to the specific case and audience.

---

## When this case type applies

**Typical prompts:**
- "How should [NGO] address [poverty / education / health / livelihood] in [region]?"
- "Design an intervention for [social problem]"
- "How should [foundation] allocate [grant amount] over the next [period]?"
- "Should [social venture] scale [program]?"
- "Evaluate [policy intervention] for [population]"
- "What's the most cost-effective approach to [outcome]?"
- "How should we measure impact of [program]?"

**Audience:** Foundation board, NGO leadership, policy makers, social impact investors, government program leads. Expects evidence-based thinking, beneficiary-centric perspective, awareness of unintended consequences, and humility about what works.

**Typical deliverable:** 30–60 slides. Less banker-flex than M&A; more emphasis on stakeholder voice, theory of change, and evidence.

## Section content adaptation

### II. Context = Problem / Population Analysis

Funnel from broad problem context to the specific intervention opportunity:
- Problem scale (population affected, severity, trends)
- Current state of interventions (what's been tried, what works, what doesn't)
- Root cause analysis vs symptoms
- Funder / implementer landscape
- The specific gap in current approaches (foreshadow the alternative)

### III. Actors = Beneficiaries + Implementers + Funders + Government

Standard sub-sections:
1. **Beneficiaries** — Demographics, needs, current outcomes, lived experience, agency
2. **Direct implementers** — NGOs / social enterprises / government agencies / community groups currently delivering services
3. **Funders** — Foundations, government, private donors, impact investors
4. **Government / regulatory** — Policy environment, government programs, regulatory constraints
5. **Theory-of-change alignment** — Are these actors aligned around a coherent theory of change?

### IV. Quantitative Analysis = Cost-Effectiveness + SROI + Outcomes

**A. Cost-Effectiveness Analysis:**
- Cost per outcome (cost per life saved, per QALY/DALY averted, per child educated, per dollar of income increased)
- Comparison to evidence-based benchmarks (GiveWell, J-PAL, Cochrane, peer-reviewed RCT meta-analyses)
- Sensitivity to key assumptions

**B. Social Return on Investment (SROI):**
- Outcomes valued in monetary terms where possible
- Stakeholder voice incorporated (beneficiaries, implementers, government)
- SROI ratio with appropriate discount rates and counterfactual reasoning

**C. Outcomes Analysis:**
- Theory of change diagram (inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes → impact)
- Evidence strength per causal link (strong RCT evidence / quasi-experimental / observational / theoretical)
- Counterfactual analysis: what would have happened without the intervention?

### V. Feasibility = Implementation Risk + Sustainability

Standard sub-topics:
1. **Implementation capacity** — Does the implementer have the team, infrastructure, local relationships to execute?
2. **Political / community support** — Will local government, community leaders, beneficiaries support this intervention?
3. **Sustainability** — Can the program continue after initial funding? Built-in revenue? Government adoption? Community ownership?
4. **Unintended consequences** — Crowd-out effects, dependency, behavioral substitution, distributional concerns
5. **Measurement infrastructure** — Can outcomes actually be measured? Baseline data? Counterfactual?
6. **Ethical concerns** — Consent, dignity, autonomy, equity within the beneficiary population

### VI. Recommended Alternative = Alternative Intervention Approach

Standard sub-sections:
1. **Evaluating alternatives** — 3-path framework (Direct service / Institution building / Behavior change) + 4–8 specific intervention shortlist
2. **Deep dive on chosen intervention** — Theory of change, evidence base, beneficiary fit, with 2–4 expert validations (academic researchers, evaluators, beneficiary representatives)
3. **Implementation plan** — Phasing, partnerships, measurement framework, scaling pathway, exit strategy

## Case-type-specific archetypes

### Theory of Change diagram
Horizontal flow: Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Long-term impact. Each transition labeled with assumptions and evidence strength. Often vertical labels showing which causal links have strong/weak evidence.

### Logic Model
Variant of theory of change. Inputs / Activities / Outputs / Outcomes / Impact in a table format with measurement plan for each.

### Cost-Effectiveness Frontier
Scatter chart: Cost per beneficiary (X axis) × Outcome magnitude per beneficiary (Y axis). Interventions plotted; the frontier (highest outcome at lowest cost) visible. The recommendation sits on or near the frontier.

### Population Pyramid / Beneficiary Mapping
Pyramid showing reach and depth: who's affected (broad) → who's reached by current programs (narrower) → who gets meaningful outcomes (narrowest). Identifies the gap.

### Evidence Strength Grid
Table: Intervention type (rows) × Outcome type (columns). Each cell colored to show evidence strength (Strong RCT / Moderate quasi-experimental / Weak observational / No evidence). Visualizes where there's evidence and where there's a gap.

### Stakeholder Voice / Quotes Slide
Direct quotes from beneficiaries, implementers, community leaders, evaluators about the problem and the proposed intervention. Higher persuasive weight than secondary research in social impact context.

### Counterfactual Comparison
Side-by-side comparison: with intervention vs without intervention, showing the marginal impact attributable to the intervention. Key for SROI rigor.

### Spillover / Network Effect Diagram
For programs with second-order effects: show how reach expands beyond direct beneficiaries. Common in education, health, behavior change interventions.

### Sustainability Stack
Funding sustainability over time: initial grant funding → matched funding → government adoption → community ownership / earned revenue. Each layer's likelihood and timing shown.

## The Social Impact Moneyshot

**Common formats:**
- **Cost-effectiveness waterfall** — Cost components stacked, divided by outcomes achieved, yielding cost per outcome. Compared to benchmark.
- **Lives reached × cost-per-life** — Total impact = reach × per-beneficiary impact, with cost decomposition
- **Counterfactual outcome chart** — Outcomes with intervention vs without, gap = attributable impact

Bottom of slide, large bold type:
*"Institution-building reaches 3.2M people at $11 per beneficiary vs direct aid at $84"*
or
*"Behavioral intervention averts X DALYs at $Y per DALY, vs $Z benchmark"*
or
*"Recommended intervention generates SROI of $4.20 per $1 invested vs $1.80 for current approach"*

## Common frameworks used in social impact cases

- **Theory of Change** — Causal logic from inputs to impact
- **Logic Model** — Operational version of theory of change
- **SROI (Social Return on Investment)** — Monetizing outcomes
- **Cost-Effectiveness Analysis** — Dollar per outcome
- **DALYs / QALYs** — Disability-adjusted / quality-adjusted life years (health interventions)
- **RCT (Randomized Controlled Trial) evidence** — Gold standard for causal inference
- **J-PAL / IPA / Cochrane** — Evidence repositories
- **Effective Altruism methodology** — GiveWell-style cost-effectiveness
- **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)** — UN framework, 17 goals with targets
- **Results-Based Management (RBM)** — Outputs / Outcomes / Impact
- **Most Significant Change** — Qualitative evaluation technique
- **OECD-DAC criteria** — Relevance / Coherence / Effectiveness / Efficiency / Impact / Sustainability
- **Capabilities Approach (Sen, Nussbaum)** — Beneficiary agency and freedoms

## Example contrarian recommendation patterns

| Case framing | Obvious answer | Contrarian alternative |
|---|---|---|
| "How to reduce poverty in [region]?" | Direct cash transfers / aid scaled up | Institution building (legal identity, banking access, property rights) |
| "Improve education outcomes" | More schools / teachers | Behavior change (parental engagement, peer tutoring, attendance incentives) |
| "Address [health problem]" | Curative care expansion | Preventive intervention / structural change |
| "Should we scale [working program]?" | Yes, expand to more cities | Deepen in current cities, then scale |
| "How to maximize impact with $Xm budget?" | Spread across multiple interventions | Concentrate on highest-evidence intervention; reject diversification |
| "Help [region] with [problem]" | Foreign-led intervention | Local-led intervention with foreign technical assistance |
| "Drive behavior change at scale" | Mass media campaign | Community-level peer change, structural environment redesign |

**Pattern:** The contrarian recognizes that obvious answers address symptoms not causes, neglect what local actors are saying, ignore evidence about what actually works, or scale too soon.

## Common social impact failure modes

1. **No counterfactual** — claiming outcomes without showing what would have happened anyway
2. **Output as outcome** — confusing "trained 10,000 teachers" with "improved learning outcomes"
3. **No theory of change** — recommending an intervention without explicit causal logic
4. **Ignoring beneficiary voice** — recommendation built from outside without consultation
5. **Weak evidence cited as strong** — citing one study or anecdote as if it were established evidence
6. **No sustainability plan** — program ends when grant ends
7. **Cost-effectiveness ignored** — recommending an expensive intervention without comparing to alternatives
8. **Unintended consequences ignored** — crowd-out, dependency, distributional concerns missing
9. **Saviorism / paternalism** — treating beneficiaries as passive recipients rather than agents
10. **Generic SDG-washing** — claiming alignment with SDGs without showing specific causal pathway

## Adapting deck length

- **15-slide pitch:** Front (1) + Exec (2) + Problem (2) + Theory of change (2) + Quantitative (3) + Alternative (3) + Conclusion (1) + Appendix (1)
- **30-slide case:** Full 8-section structure compressed
- **60-slide full deck:** Use the full template with social impact-specific archetypes

Always keep: theory of change diagram, evidence base, beneficiary voice, cost-effectiveness, the recommendation synthesis.
