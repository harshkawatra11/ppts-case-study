# Case Type: Policy, Trade, and International Relations

Policy / trade / IR cases involve recommending government policy actions, trade policy decisions, sanctions, foreign policy responses, or coalition strategies. Covers domestic policy, trade policy, geopolitical strategy, and international relations.

**Calibration note:** Policy patterns described here are extrapolated from the meta-playbook + welfare economics, deterrence theory, and standard policy analysis frameworks, not directly observed from a policy/IR corpus. Treat as plausible starting points; adjust to the specific case and audience.

---

## When this case type applies

**Typical prompts:**
- "Should [country] impose tariffs on [country/sector]?"
- "How should [country] respond to [geopolitical event / hostile move]?"
- "Design a sanctions package against [country/regime]"
- "What's [country]'s strategy toward [region/rival/issue]?"
- "Should [country] join [trade agreement / alliance]?"
- "Evaluate [policy proposal] for [country/region]"
- "How should [government] regulate [industry/technology]?"
- "What's the optimal response to [trade dispute]?"

**Audience:** Government policy makers, foreign ministry, trade ministry, think tank fellows, policy advisors. Expects rigorous welfare analysis, awareness of strategic interactions, deep historical context, and consideration of second-order effects.

**Typical deliverable:** 30–60 slide briefing book or PowerPoint. Tone is analytical and measured — less banker-flex than M&A, less customer-centric than marketing.

## Section content adaptation

### II. Context = Geopolitical / Economic / Historical Context

Funnel from broad context to the specific decision point:
- Historical background of the situation
- Current state of relationships (between countries, with allies, with adversaries)
- Domestic political context (constraints, opposition, public opinion)
- International institutional context (UN, WTO, regional bodies)
- The specific leverage point the alternative recommendation addresses (foreshadow)

### III. Actors = Countries + Blocs + Domestic Groups

Standard sub-sections:
1. **Primary actor (the country making the decision)** — Strategic interests, capabilities, current posture, domestic constraints
2. **Counterpart / target country** — Strategic interests, capabilities, vulnerabilities, likely response patterns
3. **Allies / coalition partners** — Aligned interests, likely support, what they bring, what they want in return
4. **Adversaries / opposing bloc** — How they're positioned, what they might do in response
5. **Domestic stakeholders** — Industries affected, political constituencies, civil society
6. **Strategic Interest Alignment** — Where interests of primary actor align/conflict with each other actor

### IV. Quantitative Analysis = Welfare Economics + Strategic Impact + Implementation Cost

**A. Welfare Economics:**
- Direct welfare impact on primary actor (GDP, employment, consumer welfare)
- Welfare impact on counterpart (cost imposed)
- Welfare impact on allies (positive spillovers)
- Welfare impact on third parties (neutrals)
- General equilibrium analysis where relevant (CGE models, partial equilibrium for sectoral analyses)

**B. Strategic Impact:**
- Strategic position improvement / degradation (game theory framing)
- Deterrence value (does the action change adversary calculations?)
- Alliance strengthening / weakening
- Norms reinforced / weakened
- Reputation effects (with allies, adversaries, neutrals)

**C. Implementation Cost:**
- Direct fiscal cost (subsidies, enforcement, administration)
- Diplomatic capital expended
- Political cost domestically (interest group opposition, electoral risk)
- Capability requirements (enforcement infrastructure, monitoring)

### V. Feasibility = Political + Diplomatic + Enforcement

Standard sub-topics:
1. **Domestic political feasibility** — Can this pass through the legislative / executive process? What's the coalition?
2. **Diplomatic feasibility** — Will allies support? Will neutrals accept? Will adversaries find a wedge to split allies?
3. **Retaliation risk** — How will the counterpart respond? Escalation scenarios.
4. **Enforcement capability** — Can the government actually monitor and enforce the policy?
5. **Reversibility** — If conditions change, can the policy be unwound? At what cost?
6. **WTO / international law compliance** — Does the action stay within institutional rules, or violate them (and accept the cost)?

### VI. Recommended Alternative = Alternative Policy Instrument

Standard sub-sections:
1. **Evaluating alternatives** — 3-path framework (Unilateral action / Coalition action / Negotiation) + 4–8 specific instrument shortlist (tariffs / sanctions / export controls / diplomatic démarche / military signaling / negotiation / strategic patience / etc.)
2. **Deep dive on chosen instrument** — Mechanism of action, theory of effect, historical precedents, with 2–4 expert validations (think tank analysts, former officials, academic experts)
3. **Implementation plan** — Phasing, sequencing, off-ramps, escalation/de-escalation pathways, coalition coordination

## Case-type-specific archetypes

### Welfare Impact Decomposition
Waterfall showing welfare impact by stakeholder group: Direct + Spillovers + Third parties = Total welfare. Each component separately quantified with sources.

### Payoff Matrix (Game Theory)
2×2 or 3×3 matrix showing payoffs to primary actor × counterpart's actions. Standard game theory diagram showing Nash equilibria, dominant strategies.

### Stakeholder Position Map
Grid: stakeholders (rows) × interests (columns). Each cell colored (Support / Neutral / Oppose). The recommendation maximizes support and minimizes opposition where it matters most.

### Escalation Ladder
Vertical ladder of escalating actions, each rung with: action description, expected adversary response, off-ramps, fallback. Common in deterrence / coercion analyses.

### Coalition Diagram
Network diagram of countries / blocs with the primary actor in the center, lines showing alignment strength, recent history, common interests. Identifies coalition partners and rival coalition.

### Trade Volume / Welfare Impact Charts
For trade cases: import/export volumes affected, prices affected, welfare triangles (consumer surplus + producer surplus changes). Standard intro microeconomics visualization.

### Sanctions Targeting Map
For sanctions cases: chart of adversary's economic structure showing which sectors / actors / financial flows the sanctions target. Reveals proportionality and targeting precision.

### Precedent Cases Table
Historical analogs: similar situations (rows) × outcome dimensions (columns: who imposed, what action, what response, what result). Identifies what worked and what didn't.

### Off-Ramp Diagram
For coercion / sanctions cases: a tree of possible escalation/de-escalation paths with decision points and triggers. Shows the team has thought through how the situation evolves.

### Stakeholder Reaction Matrix
Table: primary actor's options (rows) × each affected actor's likely reaction (columns). Shows the team modeled the reactions, not just the initial action.

## The Policy Moneyshot

**Common formats:**
- **Welfare comparison waterfall** — Welfare gain from recommended policy vs welfare loss from obvious policy
- **Strategic position comparison** — Quantified positioning metric (capability balance, alliance strength, deterrence value) before and after each option
- **Adversary cost vs domestic cost ratio** — Showing the recommended option has high adversary cost / domestic cost ratio (efficient leverage)

Bottom of slide, large bold type:
*"Targeted sanctions on sector Z reduce adversary GDP by 2.1% while imposing only 0.3% domestic cost"*
or
*"Coalition-based response delivers same deterrence effect at 40% lower diplomatic capital expenditure"*
or
*"Strategic patience preserves $X bn in welfare and Y diplomatic relationships vs immediate retaliation"*

## Common frameworks used in policy / trade / IR cases

- **Welfare Economics** — Consumer surplus, producer surplus, deadweight loss, gains from trade
- **Game Theory** — Sequential and simultaneous games, Nash equilibrium, credible commitment, signaling
- **Deterrence Theory (Schelling)** — Commitment, signaling, focal points, escalation control
- **Realist / Liberal / Constructivist IR theory** — Different lenses on international politics
- **Trade Models (Heckscher-Ohlin, Ricardian, New Trade Theory)** — Why countries trade, who gains
- **CGE / GTAP Models** — General equilibrium quantitative analysis
- **Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)** — Standard policy evaluation
- **Regulatory Impact Assessment (RIA)** — OECD / EU standard policy evaluation framework
- **Alliance Theory (Waltz, Walt)** — Why and how alliances form
- **Sanctions Theory** — When sanctions work, when they fail
- **Veto Player Theory** — Domestic political feasibility analysis
- **Two-Level Games (Putnam)** — Domestic and international constraints

## Example contrarian recommendation patterns

| Case framing | Obvious answer | Contrarian alternative |
|---|---|---|
| "Should we impose tariffs on [country/sector]?" | Yes, broad tariffs | Targeted sanctions on specific entities; preserve trade where it benefits us |
| "Respond to [hostile move]" | Retaliation in kind | Asymmetric response in different domain (financial, technological, diplomatic) |
| "Should we join [coalition]?" | Yes, join | Conditional / partial membership preserving freedom of action |
| "How to deter [adversary]?" | Threats and military signaling | Economic / financial deterrence (sanctions architecture) |
| "Counter [trade practice]" | Anti-dumping duties, WTO complaint | Building competing supply chain through industrial policy |
| "Should we negotiate [treaty]?" | Yes / no on the treaty | Negotiate but with specific carve-outs that protect critical interests |
| "How to address [transnational problem]" | Multilateral institution-led | Plurilateral coalition of willing among capable states |
| "Punish [country] for [violation]" | Comprehensive sanctions | Targeted sanctions on regime / elite while preserving humanitarian flows |

**Pattern:** The contrarian recognizes that obvious answers escalate without leverage, target the wrong actors, ignore second-order effects on allies / neutrals, or fight on terms that disadvantage the primary actor.

## Common policy / trade / IR failure modes

1. **No game theory / no adversary modeling** — assuming the adversary doesn't react
2. **Ignoring domestic political feasibility** — recommending what's optimal in theory but impossible in practice
3. **Ignoring third-party effects** — focusing only on primary actor vs counterpart, missing allies / neutrals
4. **Generic "diplomacy" without specifics** — recommending "diplomatic engagement" without specifying who, what, how
5. **No historical precedent analysis** — proposing without checking what happened when similar actions were taken
6. **No off-ramps** — committing to an action without thinking through how to de-escalate
7. **Conflating signaling with effectiveness** — measuring success by what the action *says* not what it *does*
8. **Ignoring unintended consequences** — sanctions that empower the regime, tariffs that hurt the home country more, alliances that entangle
9. **Treating the international system as static** — ignoring that other actors will adapt
10. **Moralizing without strategy** — recommending action because it's "right" without strategic justification

## Adapting deck length

- **15-slide briefing:** Front (1) + Exec (2) + Context (2) + Actors (2) + Quantitative (3) + Alternative (3) + Conclusion (1) + Appendix (1)
- **30-slide policy memo with slides:** Full 8-section structure compressed
- **60-slide briefing book:** Use the full template with policy-specific archetypes
- **Briefing book format (text-heavy):** Adapt slide patterns into page sections; meta-patterns (action titles → declarative headings, foreshadowing, scenarios, defensive depth) still apply

Always keep: stakeholder analysis, welfare/strategic impact decomposition, retaliation scenarios, the recommendation synthesis.
