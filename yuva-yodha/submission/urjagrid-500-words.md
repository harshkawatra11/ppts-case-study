On India's record-demand day this May, the grid was 2.57 GW short after sunset and only 0.18 GW short in solar hours. At the edge of the network, that evening gap is still closed by opening a feeder switch, and every home behind it goes dark. CEEW's smart meters in Mathura and Bareilly recorded homes losing about two hours of supply a day, while the utility's own feeder log showed a few hours a month.

Each year of solar growth makes the climb after sunset steeper. The gap falls hardest on low-income urban and peri-urban lanes. In the same study, homes in small towns lost more hours a day than homes at district headquarters. A feeder switch cannot tell a clinic from an air cooler, so the shop's refrigerator stops along with the child's evening study. For the DISCOM, every evening a transformer runs above its rating shortens its life.

UrjaGrid is software that puts a control point India has already paid for to work: the load-limit switch inside every smart meter, 7.24 crore of them installed. It forecasts each transformer's evening shortfall and tries softer levers first: a voluntary shift paid with a ₹2 a unit rebate, managed charging, deferral of shiftable loads, and storage where it exists. For the homes that still have to give, it proposes a time-boxed lifeline limit of 300 W, enough for a fan, lights and a phone, in place of a blackout. A named engineer approves every plan, each home gets two hours' notice, and critical homes are never touched.

What is new is that UrjaGrid limits rather than cuts, so a capped home keeps its fan while a shed home keeps nothing. The meter, not the feeder, is the unit of fairness, and a ledger rotates the burden so the same lane does not give first every time. Deterministic engines make every decision, and a language model only explains it in Hindi or English. No new hardware is needed, so it works "without requiring major capital investment", as the brief asks. The IEA finds that demand flexibility "can also deliver capacity to the grid at a cost up to three times lower than building new capacity".

We modelled the effect on the prototype's own network of 48 transformers and 7,000 connections, with the same shedding rules on both sides. On the simulated heatwave day, hours without supply per home fall from 4.2 hours to 0.4 hours in the base case, a 90% cut, at ₹383 a meter a year to the DISCOM and ₹0 to the home. Low-income homes and small shops keep a lifeline instead of darkness, and clinics and life-support homes are never capped. Engineers get a ranked plan instead of a shedding roster, and transformers spend fewer hours above their rating. UrjaGrid is a working prototype, the reliability gain is simulated, and a six-week pilot on one sub-division would measure it.
