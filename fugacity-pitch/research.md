## 1. Case selection

### 1.1 The problem statement, verbatim

"Large quantities of industrial waste are generated across manufacturing sectors every day. Many
of these waste streams contain materials, chemicals, or energy that can potentially be recovered
and put to productive use. Treating such streams only as waste can lead to resource loss, disposal
costs, and environmental concerns. How can industrial waste streams be converted into valuable
products, materials, chemicals, or energy, creating an economically viable solution while reducing
waste and environmental impact? Participants are encouraged to identify promising waste streams
and develop practical pathways for their conversion, recovery, or reuse, while considering
technical feasibility, scalability, economics, and environmental benefits."

Three requirements sit inside that prompt and each one maps to a section of this document and a
slide in the deck: identify a promising stream (Section 2, the stream screen), a practical
conversion pathway (Section 5, process engineering), and technical feasibility, scalability,
economics and environmental benefit, each argued with its own evidence base (Sections 5 through
11).

### 1.2 The stream screen

Five Indian industrial waste streams were scored on a weighted matrix before red mud was selected.
Weights: Volume (15 percent), value density of the recoverable fraction (30 percent), utilisation
gap, meaning how far the stream is from being solved already (25 percent), policy and strategic
tailwind (20 percent), and technical feasibility at pilot-to-commercial readiness (10 percent).

Formula used for the score shown on the deck's scoring-matrix panel:

`Score = 0.15 x Volume + 0.30 x ValueDensity + 0.25 x UtilisationGap + 0.20 x PolicyTailwind + 0.10 x TechFeasibility`

each factor scored 1 to 5 against the evidence in the table.

## 2. The stream, characterised

### 2.1 What red mud is

Red mud, also called bauxite residue, is the insoluble solid byproduct of the Bayer process, in
which bauxite ore is digested in hot caustic soda to extract alumina for aluminium smelting. What
remains, roughly one to two tonnes of residue per tonne of alumina produced depending on ore grade,
is a highly alkaline slurry, pH 10 to 13, carrying the iron, titanium and silica from the original
ore along with residual sodium hydroxide.
Source: sustainability and technoeconomic review literature on red mud, mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/3/1258.

### 2.2 Indian generation, by named refinery

| Refinery | Owner | Location | Red mud generated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Damanjodi | NALCO, a Government of India public sector undertaking | Odisha | 3.10 million tonnes per year |
| Utkal Alumina | Hindalco Industries | Odisha | 2.05 million tonnes per year |
| Lanjigarh | Vedanta Aluminium | Odisha | 2.00 million tonnes per year |
| Total, three refineries | | | 9.17 million tonnes per year |

Source: industry capacity reporting, alcircle.com, cross-referenced against refinery capacity
disclosures.

All three sites sit in Odisha, within roughly a 300 kilometre radius of each other. That geography
matters directly for the go-to-market cluster logic in Section 10.

Forward trajectory. Vedanta Aluminium's stated capacity plan moves from 2.5 to 6 million tonnes per
year of aluminium over the next three years. Hindalco is expanding its Aditya Aluminium complex in
Odisha by a further 360,000 tonnes per year of smelting capacity. Because red mud generation scales
with alumina throughput, 9.17 million tonnes per year is treated as a floor, not a ceiling. The
deck presents it as the base case and notes the expansion-driven upside separately, so as not to
count uncommitted capacity into the headline figure.
Source: alcircle.com capacity reporting.

Global context. Global red mud generation exceeded 100 million tonnes per year across the reported
2011 to 2018 period, and worldwide utilisation remains near 2 to 3 percent, meaning the majority is
impounded in surface ponds indefinitely. That four-decade persistence, despite an extensive
academic literature on valorisation, is itself evidence that low-value bulk-construction routes,
bricks and cement filler among them, have not solved the problem and are unlikely to.
Source: mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/3/1258, "Red Mud as a Secondary Resource of Low-Grade Iron: A Global
Perspective."

research. The deck states the assay is refinery-specific and does not assume uniformity across all
9.17 million tonnes per year.

### 2.4 The headline arithmetic. Contained, not recoverable

9.17 million tonnes per year multiplied by 55 ppm scandium gives approximately 500 tonnes of
contained scandium arising in Indian red mud ponds every year, if the Damanjodi assay is taken as
representative across all three sites. That assumption is flagged on the slide, not hidden.

This is contained metal, not recoverable metal. Actual recovery depends on the leaching and
solvent-extraction efficiency of the process, Section 5, which for red-mud scandium-recovery
routes in the literature is reported well under 100 percent, commonly cited at lab scale in a 40 to
75 percent band depending on method. This research did not find a red-mud-specific, commercial-
scale scandium recovery efficiency figure, and none is invented for the deck. Section 8's
sensitivity analysis carries recovery percentage as an explicit variable rather than assuming a
single point value.

## 3. Bottom-up market sizing. Named institutions, not a top-down slice of TAM

Consistent with this project's India-first content rule, sizing is built bottom-up from named
institution counts, not as a percentage slice of a global figure.

Serviceable addressable stream equals 9.17 million tonnes per year, the sum of three named,
currently operating refineries: NALCO Damanjodi, Hindalco Utkal, Vedanta Lanjigarh.

Serviceable obtainable market, year five, illustrative rollout target, is sized to a module
footprint processing a fraction of one refinery's annual arising in the pilot phase, then a
full-refinery module, then a multi-site cluster. Section 10's go-to-market funnel stages this
explicitly rather than presenting a single number with no path to it.

Forward addressable stream. Vedanta's stated 2.5 to 6 million tonne aluminium capacity plan and
Hindalco's 360,000 tonne Aditya expansion both imply proportionate red-mud growth. This is
directional upside, noted separately, and not counted into the base case.

No fourth or fifth refinery is assumed. Any other Indian alumina capacity announcement is excluded
from sizing unless a specific, named, commissioned facility is found, to avoid inflating the
serviceable stream with speculative capacity.

## 4. The disposal counterfactual. What the refineries pay today

This is the value PUNARdhatu sells against, and it needs the same rigour as the recovery-side
economics, not a footnote.

Pond capex and land. Red mud is stored in surface impoundments, sometimes called residue storage
areas, requiring dedicated land, embankment construction and liner systems to contain the pH 10 to
13 slurry and prevent groundwater contamination.

Ongoing operating cost. Dyke raising as the pond fills, dust suppression on the exposed dried
surface, red mud dust is a recognised community and environmental hazard around Odisha refineries,
and leachate monitoring and treatment are recurring operating items for the refinery, indefinitely.

Closure and post-closure liability. Under India's Hazardous and Other Wastes, Management and
Transboundary Movement, Rules framework, a residue impoundment carries closure and long-term
post-closure monitoring obligations that persist for years after the refinery itself may reduce
output. This is an open-ended liability on the refinery's balance sheet.

Regulatory exposure. Red mud pond breaches are a recognised global industrial-disaster category.
The 2010 Ajka, Hungary red mud spill is the reference case cited internationally, and it elevates
this from a cost-accounting issue to a corporate risk-management issue for refinery boards, which
is directly relevant to why a chief financial officer and an environment, health and safety head
both sit on the buying committee in Section 10.

Framing for the deck. The refinery's true alternative to a PUNARdhatu contract is not zero cost and
no action. It is continuing to pay to store, monitor and eventually close a growing hazardous
impoundment, indefinitely. That is the counterfactual the tolling or build-own-operate pitch is
measured against in Section 7, and it is why the customer conversation is a liability conversation,
not a commodity-sale conversation.

A quantified per-tonne disposal-avoided-cost figure specific to Indian red mud ponds was not found
in this research and must not be estimated or invented for the deck. The business model slide
presents pond capex and opex qualitatively, as avoided liability, while the quantified economics
rest on the recovered-product revenue side in Section 7, which does have a sourced technoeconomic
anchor.

## 5. Process engineering. The section a chemical-engineering jury will probe hardest

### 5.1 Overall route. A four-stage cascade, not one unit operation

| Stage | Unit operation | Feed | Product | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Dewatering and feed preparation | As-pumped red mud slurry | Thickened cake, roughly 40 to 50 percent solids, standard Bayer-plant dewatering practice | Standard red-mud handling |
| 1 | Reductive roasting plus magnetic separation | Dewatered red mud plus a carbon reductant | Magnetic concentrate, metallic iron or iron sponge, and a non-magnetic tailing | Fe2O3 at 52 weight percent is the largest recoverable oxide by mass |
| 2 | Non-magnetic fraction processing | Non-magnetic tailing from Stage 1 | TiO2-enriched concentrate | TiO2 at 4.88 weight percent |
| 3 | Acid bake or leach, then solvent extraction | Residual solids or Stage 1 and 2 leach liquor | Sc2O3 plus mixed rare-earth concentrate | 55 ppm scandium, 433 ppm sum of rare earths |
| 4 | Alkalinity neutralisation, sodium recovery | Demetallised residue | Geopolymer or supplementary-cementitious-material grade tailing, sodium liquor returned toward the Bayer circuit | Closes the loop on the pH 10 to 13 causticity |

### 5.2 Stage 1 detail. Iron recovery

Two established routes appear in the literature.

Direct reduction, the DRI route, operates at a comparatively low temperature, around 300 degrees
Celsius, and is reported as more energy and cost efficient than smelting routes, producing an iron
sponge suitable for magnetic separation.
Source: icsoba.org, "Production of Green Direct Reduced Iron from Red Mud of Indian Origin, A Novel
Concept."

Reductive smelting, the pig iron route, mixes red mud with a carbon or graphite reductant and
smelts it. One reported laboratory process achieves 71 percent iron recovery, yielding pig iron
suitable for heat-resistant castings.
Source: academia.edu, "Reductive Smelting of Neutralized Red Mud for Iron Recovery and Produced Pig
Iron for Heat-Resistant Castings."

Temperature threshold. Thermal-reduction literature distinguishes a lower-temperature regime, up to
roughly 1300 degrees Celsius, producing an iron sponge, from a higher-temperature regime above 1300
degrees Celsius producing cast iron. This threshold is a real design decision, because it trades
reductant and energy cost against product form and downstream saleability.
Source: mdpi.com/10.3390/app9224902, "Iron Recovery from Bauxite Tailings Red Mud by Thermal
Reduction with Blast Furnace Sludge."

Recommendation for the deck, stated as a design trade-off, not a settled fact. DRI-style low-
temperature reduction is the more energy-efficient route and better suited to a distributed
refinery-gate module, so Stage 1 in the cascade is pitched as DRI-based. Full smelting to pig iron
is a higher-capex, higher-technology-readiness alternative appropriate for a later, consolidated
stage of the rollout.

### 5.3 Stage 3 detail. Scandium and rare-earth recovery

The literature is consistent that scandium in red mud concentrates in specific mineral
associations, and that selective leaching followed by solvent extraction is the standard laboratory
approach. This typically uses an alkali-fusion or acid-bake pretreatment ahead of leaching, to break
down the refractory mineral matrix, followed by solvent-extraction reagents selective for Sc3+ over
the co-dissolved Fe3+ and Al3+, which are present at far higher concentration and would otherwise
overwhelm a non-selective extractant.
Source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8779884, "Selective Scandium Extraction from Bauxite
Residue Obtained by Alkali Fusion-Leaching Method."

Technology readiness, stated honestly. Scandium and rare-earth recovery from red mud sits at
laboratory to pilot scale globally. This research did not identify a commercial-scale, continuously
operating scandium-from-red-mud plant anywhere in the world. NALCO's own red-mud pilot plant,
targeting ferro-titanium alloy, metallic iron and rare earths, is explicitly a pilot, not a
commercial reference plant.
Sources: alcircle.com, "NALCO explores rare earth and valuable material recovery from bauxite
residue and fly ash"; pib.gov.in, National Critical Mineral Mission press materials.

This is represented on the deck's technology-readiness ladder, Stage 1 closer to pilot or near-
commercial via the DRI precedent, Stage 3 explicitly lab-to-pilot. Presenting scandium recovery as
commercially proven would be a fabrication under the competition's rules and is not done anywhere
in this deck.

### 5.4 Stage 4. Alkalinity and the closed loop

Neutralising the residual sodium hydroxide and returning recovered sodium toward the Bayer circuit
both reduces the causticity hazard of the final tailing, making it viable as a geopolymer or
supplementary-cementitious-material feedstock, which needs a far less alkaline input than raw red
mud, and creates a small internal credit against the refinery's own caustic soda consumption. This
is a real but minor economic and environmental line item, not a headline number.

### 5.5 Economic anchor for the whole cascade

A published technoeconomic study on an integrated separation-extraction process for red mud reports
an economic return of approximately 138 US dollars per tonne of red mud processed, alongside
approximately 60 percent stockpile mass reduction.
Source: sciencedirect.com S2214993724002331, "Technologies for recovery of iron from red mud,
Processes, challenges and opportunities."

This is the single most important economic anchor in this research base. It is a published, third-
party technoeconomic figure, not a number this team derived, and it is cited exactly as such on the
business-model slide and in the appendix, with the caveat that it reflects one study's assumed
process route, reagent costs and product prices, which may not transfer directly to the Odisha
context without adjustment. That caveat is a flagged assumption, not a promise.

## 6. Technology readiness ladder. Stated honestly

| Stage | Technology | Readiness band | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dewatering and feed preparation | Standard Bayer-plant practice | Commercial | Already practiced at every alumina refinery |
| Iron recovery, DRI route | Low-temperature reductive roasting plus magnetic separation | Pilot to near-commercial | Concept-stage processes reported in the literature. No continuous commercial red-mud-specific plant identified |
| TiO2 recovery | Non-magnetic fraction processing | Lab to pilot | Reported as part of integrated recovery studies |
| Scandium and rare-earth recovery | Alkali-fusion or acid-bake leach plus solvent extraction | Lab to pilot | No commercial-scale plant identified globally. NALCO's own effort is explicitly a pilot |
| Alkalinity neutralisation, geopolymer tailing | De-alkalisation plus supplementary-cementitious-material formulation | Lab to pilot, on a more mature geopolymer-from-red-mud literature base | Red-mud geopolymer literature is comparatively more developed than the metal-recovery steps |

This ladder is itself a slide component. It is evidence of rigour, presented directly, and it
pre-empts the jury's most likely challenge, has anyone actually done this at scale, with a direct
answer: not yet, and the deck says so.

## 7. Unit economics

Given the technology-readiness status above, unit economics are built as a scenario range anchored
to the one published third-party technoeconomic figure in Section 5.5, not as a bottom-up capex and
opex model built from unverified assumptions. Inventing detailed capex or opex line items without a
sourced basis would breach the competition's no-fabricated-data rule.

Anchor. Approximately 138 US dollars per tonne of red mud processed, approximately 60 percent
stockpile reduction, sourced in Section 5.5.

Revenue architecture, by cascade stage, per Section 2's staged table: iron is the ballast, high
mass and modest margin. Titanium dioxide is the secondary line. Scandium and rare earths are the
margin layer, low mass and high value density. The de-alkalised residue is an avoided-disposal-cost
credit, not a sold product.

Structural claim, sourced from the market data in Section 8. Because Sc2O3 trades in the 1,200 to
5,500-plus US dollar per kilogram range against iron and titanium dioxide trading at commodity-
level dollar-per-tonne prices, a very small mass fraction, the approximately 55 ppm scandium, can
plausibly contribute a disproportionate share of module revenue. This is presented as a directional
structural argument backed by the price ratio, not as a precise modelled revenue split, because no
sourced per-tonne recovery-cost breakdown by stage was found.

Business model choice. Build-own-operate or tolling to the refinery, not commodity mining and
selling. This is a strategic argument, Section 2, not a quantified line item, and is presented on
the business-model slide as the chosen structure with its stated rationale, a liability-based sale
that insulates the refinery's obligation, though not PUNARdhatu's own margin, from scandium price
volatility. That margin exposure is explicitly flagged as a risk in Section 12.

What the deck must not do. Present a fabricated capex figure, a fabricated opex breakdown, or a
fabricated multi-year revenue or EBITDA table with invented precision. Financial slides show the
sourced anchor, the scenario logic and the sensitivity variables in Section 8, framed honestly as
an illustrative model built on one published technoeconomic reference, the same honesty this
project's standing rules require when primary data does not exist.

## 8. Scandium and rare-earth market, including the constraint the deck must not hide

| Metric | Value | Source class |
|---|---|---|
| Global Sc2O3 production, 2025 | Approximately 80 tonnes, up from a historical 30 to 40 tonnes per year | Market-tracker aggregation |
| Global Sc2O3 consumption, 2025 | Approximately 60 tonnes | Same class |
| Sc2O3 price, pre-export-controls | Approximately 1,200 US dollars per kilogram | Same class |
| Sc2O3 price, post-controls, spot | 3,500 to 5,500 US dollars per kilogram | Same class |
| Sc2O3 price, 2025 average | Approximately 5,737 US dollars per kilogram, on approximately 41 tonnes of global sales volume | Same class |
| Forecast demand, 2026 to 2027 | 117-plus tonnes per year, driven by aluminium-scandium alloy for aerospace and solid-oxide fuel cell adoption | Same class |
| China's share | Approximately 85 percent of refined supply, approximately 100 percent of metallised scandium | Same class |
| Non-Chinese scale supply | Not expected before 2027 to 2029 | Same class |

Market-tracker sources cross-referenced for this table: lanthanides.io, scarceearth.io,
rare-earth-mining.com, criticalstrategicmetals.com, guidechem.com, metal.com, businessanalytiq.com.
These are treated as directional market data, not audited financial figures, and are flagged as
such wherever they appear on a slide.

The constraint, stated plainly. India's approximately 500 tonnes per year of contained scandium,
Section 2.4, is roughly six times the entire 2025 world production of refined scandium oxide. This
is not a case for claiming India could instantly capture that value. It is the opposite. It is
structurally impossible to sell 500 tonnes per year of scandium into an approximately 60 to 80
tonne per year world market without collapsing the price. The deck addresses this directly rather
than presenting the 500-tonne figure as straightforwardly monetisable. Two honest responses belong
on the slide.

First, recovery is partial, not total. Actual recoverable scandium per year, at any realistic
process efficiency and any realistic build-out pace, is a small fraction of the 500 tonne per year
contained figure, phased in over years, not captured in year one.

Second, the build-out itself should be phased against demonstrated market absorption. Start at a
scale that is a rounding error against world production, proving the process and securing offtake,
and let capacity expansion track observed price and demand response, not the full contained-
resource figure.

This thin-market absorption point appears first in the risk register, Section 12, and is answered,
not sidestepped, in the go-to-market staging.

Strategic tailwind, kept separate from the market-size constraint. China named scandium in its 2025
export-control expansions, Section 9, which is a geopolitical and supply-security argument for why
India should build domestic scandium capability at all, independent of whether the market can
absorb India's full theoretical contained resource. These are two different arguments and are not
conflated on any slide. One is "there is a market opportunity." The other is "there is a strategic
security reason to build this capability regardless of market size."

## 9. Policy and strategic layer

National Critical Mineral Mission. Approved by Cabinet, announced in the Union Budget 2024-25
context, formally covering FY24-25 to FY30-31, with a proposed outlay of 16,300 crore rupees in
central expenditure and an expected further 18,000 crore rupees investment from public sector
undertakings and other stakeholders. The mission's scope explicitly extends across the value chain,
exploration, mining, beneficiation, processing and recycling of critical minerals.
Sources: pib.gov.in press releases on the National Critical Mineral Mission; iea.org policy
database entry.

Unconventional sources, named explicitly. National Critical Mineral Mission policy documents
identify overburden, mine tailings, fly ash and red mud as unconventional sources for
critical-mineral recovery, with approximately 100 crore rupees set aside specifically for pilot
projects in this category, alongside relaxed rules and incentives to promote secondary-source
mineral recovery.
Sources: mongabay-india.com reporting on the mission's approval; mines.gov.in mission presentation
deck.

NALCO's own pilot programme. NALCO has developed a red-mud pilot plant targeting recovery of
ferro-titanium alloy, metallic iron and rare earth elements, alongside a parallel fly-ash pilot
recovering alumina, calcium silicate, quartz residue and a scandium-enriched iron-hydroxide
residue. This is direct evidence that the opportunity is real enough for a major Indian public
sector undertaking to invest in it, and that NALCO is simultaneously a potential anchor partner or
customer, it already generates 3.10 million tonnes per year, the largest single source, and a
potential competitor on the scandium and rare-earth recovery intellectual property itself. The
competitive-landscape section, Section 13, takes a clear position on which.
Sources: alcircle.com, psuwatch.com reporting.

Private-sector entrant. Enervoxa, described as a Canadian climate-technology company, has publicly
discussed a commercial-scale rare-earth-processing plant in India recovering critical minerals from
red mud, and has been reported seeking partnerships with Vedanta, Hindalco and NALCO. This is
evidence of external validation of the opportunity, and a named competitor or potential partner for
the competitive-landscape slide.
Sources: alcircle.com, "Enervoxa eyes Indian rare earths plant from red mud"; discoveryalert.com.au
reporting on the Enervoxa project.

China's export-control expansion, 2025. In April 2025, China introduced stricter export controls on
rare-earth magnets requiring end-user certification and special licenses. In October 2025 it
expanded the controlled-element list to add holmium, erbium, thulium, europium and ytterbium, and
separately, controls target terbium, yttrium, dysprosium, gadolinium, lutetium, samarium and
scandium, plus magnets made from these materials. Chinese customs data show a 58 percent decline in
rare-earth magnet exports to India since January 2025.
Sources: vifindia.org, indrastra.com, deccanherald.com reporting.

India's import dependence. India imported 93 percent of its rare-earth magnets from China in
FY24-25, with reported stockpiles lasting only two to three weeks after the restrictions began
biting, a genuine supply-security vulnerability, not a hypothetical one.
Sources: openthemagazine.com, sabiduriacapital.substack.com reporting.

## 10. Go to market

Funnel logic, bottom-up and named-institution-based, consistent with Section 3: three named
refineries generating 9.17 million tonnes per year, filtered to sites passing a defined technical
and commercial screen, staged into modules commissioned over a five-year build-out.

Proposed five-point site screen. This is a design choice this team applied to the three known
refineries, and would apply to any future site, not an external published standard.

One. Minimum annual red-mud arising sufficient to amortise a module, which favours larger sites
first, Damanjodi's 3.10 million tonnes per year is the natural anchor site.

Two. Existing on-site or co-located caustic or Bayer infrastructure, so recovered sodium liquor can
be returned into the existing circuit, Section 5.4, without new transport infrastructure.

Three. Grid power and process-water availability already present, true of all three sites as
operating alumina refineries.

Four. Refinery-level appetite for a liability-transfer or build-own-operate structure, evidenced by
NALCO's own pilot-plant investment, Section 9, as a signal of institutional appetite.

Five. Proximity to Odisha's existing industrial-chemical and metallurgical ecosystem for offtake
logistics. All three sites are within Odisha.

Buying committee, named roles, not invented personas.

| Role | Needs | Lacks today | Gets from PUNARdhatu |
|---|---|---|---|
| Refinery or plant head | Reduced pond footprint, lower closure liability | An economically self-funding path to residue reduction | A build-own-operate partner that reduces stockpile at no new capex burden |
| Chief financial officer | A liability moved off the balance sheet, or converted to revenue-linked operating cost | A commercially proven route to monetise a waste stream | A tolling or build-own-operate structure with a defined fee basis |
| Environment, health and safety head | Reduced dust, leachate and closure risk | A technical partner for alkalinity and dust mitigation | The de-alkalisation step, Section 5.4, as direct risk reduction |
| Corporate sustainability | A quantifiable circular-economy and ESG narrative | A credible, third-party-validated recovery pathway | A cited, National Critical Mineral Mission-aligned recovery story |
| State pollution control board, Odisha | Confidence that a new process at the refinery gate meets Hazardous Waste Rules and reduces, rather than adds, environmental risk | This is the approver, not a beneficiary | An avenue to demonstrably reduce impounded hazardous-waste volume, aligned with the Central Pollution Control Board's own objectives |

Anchor-then-expand path. Start at NALCO Damanjodi, the largest arising, public sector alignment
with the National Critical Mineral Mission, and existing pilot-plant precedent to build credibility
from. Expand to the other two Odisha refineries, Hindalco Utkal and Vedanta Lanjigarh, once the
anchor module demonstrates recovery performance. Longer term, address legacy stockpile, decades of
already-impounded red mud at all three sites, a larger but harder-to-access resource, and consider
licensing the process package to refineries outside India facing the same more-than-100-million-
tonne-per-year global problem.

## 11. Environmental impact, presented net, not gross

Avoided-impoundment benefit. Every tonne processed through the cascade is a tonne that does not
require new pond capacity, reducing land take and the long-run closure and monitoring liability
described in Section 4.

Alkalinity neutralised. The Stage 4 de-alkalisation step directly addresses the pH 10 to 13 hazard
that makes red mud pond breaches, the 2010 Ajka, Hungary incident being the reference case
internationally, a recognised catastrophic-risk category.

Import substitution. Domestically recovered scandium and rare earths reduce reliance on the
93-percent-China-sourced rare-earth-magnet supply chain, Section 9, a strategic environmental and
security co-benefit distinct from tonnage-based metrics.

Honest net-energy framing, and this is not skipped. The cascade itself is energy intensive.
Reductive roasting, acid leaching and solvent extraction all consume reagents and thermal or
electrical energy. A credible carbon-dioxide-equivalent claim requires netting the cascade's own
energy and reagent-production footprint against the emissions avoided by displacing virgin iron ore
mining and smelting, virgin titanium feedstock production, and virgin scandium production, itself
typically a coproduct of other rare-earth or uranium processing with its own environmental
footprint, plus avoided pond-related land degradation. No net carbon-dioxide-equivalent-per-tonne
figure was found for this specific process route, and none is invented for the deck. The deck
presents the logic of the net calculation, avoided virgin production minus process energy, as a
stated framework, and marks the actual net number as to be quantified at pilot stage. That is an
honest, disqualification-safe way to handle a real analytical gap.

## 13. Competitive landscape and the actual moat

NALCO. Simultaneously the largest potential anchor customer, 3.10 million tonnes per year arising,
and a potential direct competitor on scandium and rare-earth recovery intellectual property, given
its own announced pilot plant. Position for the deck: partner, not compete. NALCO's pilot validates
the opportunity and de-risks the technical thesis. PUNARdhatu's differentiation is the multi-
refinery, build-own-operate or tolling business model, not a claim to superior or proprietary
chemistry NALCO's own scientists have not already investigated.

Enervoxa. A named external entrant reportedly seeking partnerships with all three Odisha refineries
for red-mud rare-earth-element recovery. This is direct evidence of investor and market conviction
in the opportunity, and a named competitor for exclusive refinery relationships. The deck is candid
that securing an anchor refinery partnership is itself a competitive, time-sensitive action, not a
guaranteed outcome.

Global bauxite-residue ventures, historically. The approximately 2 to 3 percent global utilisation
rate after four decades of academic and some commercial attempts, Section 2.2, is itself evidence
that most prior ventures in this space have not achieved durable commercial scale. The deck does
not claim PUNARdhatu is certain to succeed where others have not. It argues the structural
difference, a cascade that is not dependent on any single product, and a liability-based sale to
the refinery rather than a commodity-price-dependent sale, as the reason this attempt is
differently positioned, not guaranteed.

The actual moat, stated plainly. First, refinery-gate siting reduces transport cost to effectively
zero for the input, a claim no bricks, cement or road-base competitor can make once freight radius,
Section 2.2, is accounted for. Second, the build-own-operate or tolling structure aligns incentives
with the refinery's own balance-sheet liability rather than competing purely on commodity price.
Third, the cascade design means a single product-price collapse, scandium oxide falling if China
relaxes controls, for example, does not zero out the business, because iron and titanium dioxide
revenue continue independently.
