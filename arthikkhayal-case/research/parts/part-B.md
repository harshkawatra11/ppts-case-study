## 4. Indian equities had a weak year that hit first-time investors hardest, and ONL's 14.74% sits below India's active ratio of about one in five

### 4.10 Where ONL sits against this national picture, and the gap it leaves open

ONL's activation rate moved from 14.70% to 14.74% across the year to September 2026 [C-E1], a rise of 0.04 percentage points, while the national active ratio computed in section 4.1 sits at roughly 19%, [C01, C10, C12]. The two rates are not perfectly comparable (ONL's spans its own 12-month trailing trade count on its own account base; the national ratio spans every broker and every investor type at every price point), but the gap between them, on the order of four to five percentage points, is large enough to survive that caveat.

The products that held up nationally through a falling market, SIPs and gold ETFs [C51 to C61], share a mechanism: a customer decides once, on a schedule they set, and the product then runs without requiring them to read the market and choose a moment. The products that fell, direct equity trading and F&O [C64 to C67], share the opposite mechanism: each transaction is a fresh decision, made at a moment the customer has to notice and act on. ONL's 7,33,855-to-8,09,746-account base [C-E1] is overwhelmingly salary customers, 77.5% of it by September 2026 [T2], people whose relationship with the parent bank is already built around a recurring, scheduled event: the salary credit. What the national data shows working, and what ONL's own account base is already built to receive, point at the same kind of answer. ## 5. Bank-led brokers grow through the parent bank's channels, and ONL's parent bank already serves most of the customers behind its 8,09,746 accounts

### 5.3 VRIO: why these assets are a source of advantage, and why ONL's gap is access to them rather than the product itself

VRIO is a standard framework for internal-capability analysis: an asset is a source of competitive advantage only if it is Valuable, Rare, hard to Imitate, and the organisation is actually Organised to exploit it. Table 5.4 applies the four tests to the assets named in section 5.2, from ONL's position as the broking arm linked to a parent bank.

| Asset | Valuable | Rare | Inimitable | Organised (ONL, today) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Salary-credit timing and amount | yes, a known moment the customer is thinking about money | yes, a discount broker with no banking licence cannot see this at all | yes, copying it would require a banking relationship, which a feature alone cannot substitute for | not yet, no salary-day moment in ONL's own channel is described in the case's list of tactics tried [C-PB3 to C-PB5] | unexploited advantage |
| A savings app opened daily for routine reasons | yes, attention is the scarce resource every lever in this plan is chasing | yes, a standalone trading app competes for attention only when the customer thinks about investing | moderately, a discount broker could build engagement features, but not a banking relationship | not yet, ONL's communication ran through WhatsApp, email, RCS and social messages, not an owned bank-app surface [C-PB3] | unexploited advantage |
| RM relationships on premium tiers | yes, for the four premium cohorts specifically | yes, relative to a discount app with no human relationship manager | moderately, a wealth-tech competitor could hire RMs, but not inherit an existing banking relationship | partially, the case names research calls as a tactic tried, but not a structured RM review script [C-PB3] | partially exploited |

The pattern across this table is consistent: the assets with the clearest "valuable, rare, inimitable" profile, salary-credit timing and the savings app's daily attention, are the two ONL is least organised to use today, on the evidence of what the case says it already tried [C-PB3 to C-PB5]. The one asset ONL already exploits well, pre-verified KYC, explains why account opening is not the bottleneck [C-F1]; it does not explain the activation gap, because the gap sits later in the funnel, at login and funding [C-F2, C-F3].

## 7. Regulation allows all seven levers when messages state facts, customers choose the product and no reward is tied to a trade, and it rules out five tactics

**Answer.** Every rule found in this research permits a message that states a fact, lets the customer act or not act, and ties no reward to the act of trading, which is the design every lever in section 3.4 follows. The rules rule out five specific tactics instead: mass research calls framed as recommendations without the required disclosures, first-trade cash offers, contests and leaderboards, return-led content, and any promotion of futures and options to a loss-making segment. One genuine open question remains unresolved by any regulator text found in this research: whether a rule-built, no-default, objective-criteria list of exchange-traded funds, the mechanism behind the salary-day lever, sits inside the stockbroker's incidental-advice exemption under the Investment Advisers Regulations, and this document treats that as an open question requiring a formal request for guidance before the salary-day lever ships, not as a settled legal fact.

### 7.1 Investment advice and the incidental-advice carve-out remain an open question

SEBI's Investment Advisers Regulations, 2013 define investment advice broadly, as advice on investing in, buying, selling or dealing in securities or investment products, including portfolio advice [D01]. The definition carries an exception for advice given through a widely available medium such as a newspaper, magazine or broadcast [D02]. Separately, a stockbroker registered under the 1992 Stock Brokers Regulations is exempt from Investment Adviser registration where the advice given is incidental to the broker's main business, and only if the broker still meets Chapter III's general obligations [D03].

Table 7.1 sets out exactly what is and is not answered on this point.

| Question | Answer found | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| Whether SEBI's IA Regulations definition of advice covers a recommendation to buy a specific security | yes, explicitly | [D01] |
| Whether advice through a widely available medium (newspaper, broadcast) is excluded | yes | [D02] |
| Whether a registered stockbroker can give some advice without separate IA registration | yes, if the advice is incidental to the broker's main business and Chapter III obligations are met | [D03] |
| Whether a rule-built, objective-criteria, no-default list of ETF categories (the salary-day lever's mechanism) counts as "incidental" advice, or as advice requiring separate registration | not answered by any SEBI circular, FAQ or informal guidance found in this research | [D05] |

This is the single most consequential open question in this section. The salary-day lever (section 12 below) is built specifically to avoid being a recommendation: it offers a category list built by fixed, objective rules (gold, liquid and broad-index ETF categories), with no default selection, shown in a fixed alphabetical order, and no stated return expectation. That design choice is a direct response to the uncertainty in Table 7.1, not a workaround of it. Because no primary source resolves the question, this document's recommendation, repeated in section 5.3 of the plan this research supports, is that ONL request informal guidance from SEBI on this specific mechanism before the salary-day lever goes live, rather than proceed on the team's own reading of Regulation 4(g).

### 7.2 Research Analyst rules and mass research calls

SEBI's Research Analyst Regulations took effect 1 December 2014, and require anyone acting as a research analyst, or holding out as one, to register unless specifically exempt [D07]. Regulation 21 requires a person making a public appearance or recommendation through public media to disclose their name, registration status and any financial interest in the company discussed [D08]. SEBI's own FAQ excludes clerical, marketing and back-office staff from the definition [D09]. No circular or FAQ found in this research defines "mass research calls" as a separate, named regulated category distinct from ordinary research distribution [D11]; the case's own phrase, "successful research calls" sent over WhatsApp, email, RCS and social media [C-PB3], falls under the same public-media disclosure duty in Regulation 21 as any other research distribution, not a separate rule written specifically for bulk messaging.

### 7.3 Finfluencer regulations and the education carve-out

SEBI amended the Intermediaries Regulations on 29 August 2024, inserting Regulation 16A, which bars a SEBI-regulated entity from any direct or indirect association with an unregistered person who makes securities recommendations [D12]. An education-only carve-out survives inside this ban, where the person makes no recommendation and no claim about returns or performance [D13]. A follow-up circular dated 29 January 2025 clarified that education-focused commentators may use stock price data only with a three-month lag [D14]. None of ONL's seven levers involves an unregistered third party making a recommendation, so Regulation 16A does not restrict the levers directly; it is relevant mainly as a reminder that any workplace-education session (the sessions lever, section 17) must stay inside the education carve-out and avoid anything that reads as a recommendation.

### 7.4 NSE and BSE advertisement codes, with the status of the inducement circular corrected

NSE Circular 14/2023 (NSE/COMP/55482), dated 2 February 2023, issued the current Code of Advertisement for stock brokers [D16]. The code defines "advertisement" broadly, covering any communication by or for a broker, in any publicly available media, that could influence an investment or trading decision, including communication sent to the broker's own registered clients [D17]. It requires a standard market-risk warning, the broker's name, address and registration details, and bars the exchange's own name, logo or any celebrity appearance [D19]. Repeated violations carry escalating penalties: three unapproved advertisements bar new-client onboarding for a month; five withdraw trading rights for a working day or until approval is obtained [D20]. The exact current wording of the standard risk line was not retrieved verbatim from a primary circular in this research [D21]; no customer copy in this document quotes a specific risk-line wording for this reason, and any live draft must pull the current NSE or BSE text directly before going to compliance.

**This research corrects one citation from the planning-time note.** NSE's referral-restriction circular, NSE/INSP/63425, dated 14 August 2024, is in abeyance. NSE Circular No. 06/2025 (NSE/INSP/66284), dated 24 January 2025, suspended it pending a proposal from the Brokers' Industry Standards Forum on referrals by unregistered persons [D26]. This document's inducement argument therefore rests on the NSE advertisement code (section 7.4 above) and the general SEBI and NSE anti-inducement principle visible across the code of advertisement and the inactive-account circular (section 7.5), not on NSE/INSP/63425 as settled current law.

SEBI issued a consultation paper on a common advertisement code on 23 June 2026, proposing to fold the separate rules for brokers, depository participants, investment advisers, research analysts, online bond platforms, portfolio managers and mutual funds into one rulebook, with comments due 14 July 2026 [D22]. That consultation paper proposed replacing prior approval for ordinary advertisements with post-issuance reporting within 24 hours [D23]. **This research corrects the reporting window cited at planning time.** A later SEBI board memorandum, dated September 2026, instead proposes reporting through supervisory-body portals within three working days of issuance, not 24 hours [D24]. Neither the 24-hour figure nor the three-working-day figure is yet final law; the common advertisement code has not been issued as a binding rule as of this research's access date. Where this document or the deck needs to state a reporting window, it uses three working days as the more current figure, with the explicit note that it is not yet in force [D24].

### 7.5 The inactive-account circular, translated into the case's own terms

NSE Circular NSE/INSP/64718, dated 25 October 2024, set the current rule that a client who has not traded for 24 months is flagged by the exchange's own term, "inactive" [D29]. **Translation note, applied throughout this document:** the case defines "Inactive" differently, as an account deactivated for fraud or a regulator's notice [C-D3], and "Dormant" as the 24-month no-trade condition [C-D4]. Wherever a regulatory source uses the exchange's word "inactive" for the 24-month condition, this document uses the case's word, "dormant [C-D4]," instead, to avoid the two different meanings colliding on the page.

Reactivating a dormant-flagged account requires in-person verification or video in-person verification, per SEBI's KYC Master Circular of 12 October 2023, plus confirmation that the customer's address, mobile number, email or bank and depository details have not changed [D30]. A predecessor circular, NSE/INSP/49743 (2021), bars any communication that urges a client to trade only to avoid being flagged dormant [C-D4] [D31]. This is the specific rule the warm-cohort defence lever (section 16) is built to respect: its copy never frames a trade as a way to "stay active," only as a fact about the account and an option the customer can take or leave.

A SEBI circular dated 10 June 2024 withdrew an earlier plan to freeze the demat accounts of existing investors who had not submitted a nomination choice [D40]. ### 7.10 The Rs 250 SIP remains a proposal, with no final policy published

SEBI's January 2025 consultation paper proposed a Rs 250 small-ticket SIP, available in any scheme except debt, sectoral and thematic, and small-cap and mid-cap equity schemes, with discounted distributor economics limited to an investor's first three such SIPs across up to three fund houses [D52]. As of a report found in this research, AMFI was still collecting fund-house rollout plans as of a letter dated 27 June, and the final SEBI circular implementing the proposal had not been issued [D53]. **Status: NOT YET PUBLISHED.** At least one fund house, Kotak, separately launched its own Rs 250 "Choti SIP" ahead of any industry-wide SEBI mandate, in the Growth option only, requiring a minimum of 60 monthly instalments [D54]. ### 7.11 The digital gold caution and the SEBI-regulated alternative

SEBI's press release PR No. 70/2025, dated 8 November 2025, cautioned investors that unregulated "digital gold" or "e-gold" products are neither notified securities nor regulated commodity derivatives, and carry none of the investor-protection cover that SEBI-regulated products carry [D55]. The same release points investors instead to gold exchange-traded funds, exchange-traded commodity derivatives and Electronic Gold Receipts, all bought through SEBI-registered intermediaries [D56]. One secondary summary also lists Sovereign Gold Bonds as a SEBI-recommended alternative; Sovereign Gold Bonds are an RBI and government debt instrument, not a SEBI-regulated securities product, so this document treats that listing as a likely secondary-source error and does not cite Sovereign Gold Bonds as a SEBI-endorsed alternative in any deck copy [D56]. The Dhanteras lever (section 15) is built specifically around the SEBI-regulated gold ETF, not digital gold, and its draft copy names the SEBI caution date explicitly as the reason for the distinction.

### 7.12 UPI blocked-amount trading and UPI Autopay limits

A SEBI circular dated 11 November 2024 requires Qualified Stock Brokers to offer clients either a UPI block mechanism or a 3-in-1 trading account facility, effective 1 February 2025 [D57]. Under the UPI block mechanism, a client's funds stay in the client's own bank account, similar to the IPO ASBA design, and are debited only once the clearing corporation confirms the trade, earning interest in the meantime [D58]. NPCI began a beta of this mechanism for secondary-market equity cash trading on 1 January 2024, initially for HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank customers [D59]. UPI AutoPay itself launched in 2020, letting a customer set up a recurring mandate up to Rs 2,000 without a fresh authentication step each time; above that amount, the customer must authorise each execution with a UPI PIN [D60]. A December 2023 NPCI change raised the no-extra-authentication threshold for recurring payments to listed merchant categories from Rs 15,000 to Rs 1,00,000 [D61]. The salary-day lever's Autopay mandate design sits comfortably inside these thresholds for a typical monthly ETF instalment amount, so no extra authentication step should interrupt the recurring purchase once the mandate is set.

### 7.13 RBI directions on banks and the 2027 conduct amendment, outside the build window

RBI's Commercial Banks, Undertaking of Financial Services Directions 2025 (RBI/DOR/2025-26/148), issued 28 November 2025, take effect immediately, except paragraphs 30 to 36, which start 1 January 2026 [D63]. A December 2025 amendment clarifies that core banking stays departmental, while mutual fund, insurance, pension and portfolio-management services must run through group entities [D64]. ## 8. Inattention explains more of the inactivity than fear does, which is why a year of "stay invested" messages moved the rate by 0.04 points

**Answer.** The behavioural evidence gathered in this research points toward inattention as the larger force keeping accounts dormant [C-D4], more than fear of losses. Investors check their accounts less often after bad market news and during high volatility, the opposite of the anxious, loss-focused customer a "stay invested" reassurance campaign is built to calm [E38, E39, E41]. SEBI's own 2025 survey finds 63% of households aware of at least one securities instrument, but only 9.5% actually participating [E64], a gap that looks like attention and access more than fear specifically. This is why a year of messages built around reassurance moved ONL's own activation rate by only 0.04 percentage points [C-E1]: the message answered a worry the data suggests was not the main obstacle.

### 8.1 Limited attention as the dominant force

DellaVigna's 2009 survey of psychology and economics in the field groups limited attention together with menu effects, persuasion and social pressure, and emotion as the four categories of non-standard decision-making with the strongest field evidence behind them [E46, E47]. Separately, payday-specific research finds people are considerably more likely to log in and check their finances on payday itself, and that attention is lower when spending or overdrafts are high and higher when cash holdings and savings are high [E51]. Scarcity research finds inattention peaks just before the next income arrives, when cognitive load from financial strain is highest, not necessarily just after a loss [E52]. Read together, these findings point at a specific moment when attention to a secondary financial account, such as an ONL account linked to a salary account, should be highest: right at salary credit, not at an arbitrary date a broker chooses to send a message.

### 8.2 The ostrich effect, with the nuance that inattention does not mean more trading later

Karlsson, Loewenstein and Seppi's "ostrich effect" research models information-seeking as a function of expected good or bad news, predicting that people check their accounts less often after bad news [E37]. Using login data from Swedish pension holders and American retirement-account holders, the authors find a 1% rise in recent market returns raises the mean daily number of logins by 5% to 6% in the US data [E38]. Sicherman, Loewenstein, Seppi and Utkus's 2016 follow-up, using Vanguard retirement-account login data, finds logins drop by 9.5% after market declines [E39], an effect stronger for men, older investors and wealthier investors [E40].

**The nuance this document must not lose:** the same research finds that investors who show this ostrich-like attention avoidance after a downturn are also less likely to trade in that period [E41]. Lower attention after bad news coincides with lower trading, not a backlog of pent-up trades waiting to be released. This matters directly for ONL's situation: a year of market weakness [section 4.4] should be expected to suppress both attention and trading together, which is consistent with the flat 14.70%-to-14.74% activation line [C-E1], not a sign that customers were waiting, unnoticed, to trade once reassured. A 2014 replication attempt on a different sample of active online investors, the "Meerkat Effect," found checking rose after both large up days and large down days, and that the individual trait of neuroticism predicted checking frequency more than the direction of the news did, a result that does not fully replicate the simple ostrich pattern [E42]; this document treats the ostrich finding as the better-supported general pattern for a largely first-time, dormant [C-D4] customer base, while noting the Meerkat result as a reason not to overstate how uniform the effect is across every investor type.

### 8.3 Loss aversion and mental accounting

Kahneman and Tversky's 1979 prospect theory finds that losses loom larger than equivalent gains, a steeper value function on the loss side than the gain side [E31, E32]. A commonly cited loss-aversion coefficient from later work puts this at roughly 2 to 2.25, losses felt about twice as strongly as an equivalent gain, though a 2018 review argues much of the early supporting evidence for that specific number was over-interpreted [E33]; this document treats "losses feel considerably worse than equivalent gains" as well supported, and the specific multiple of exactly two to 2.25 as a contested number, not a precise constant. Benartzi and Thaler's 1995 work on myopic loss aversion combines loss aversion with frequent portfolio checking to explain why the long-run equity premium looks larger than standard models predict, implying investors behave as though they evaluate their portfolios on roughly a one-year cycle [E34, E35, E36].

Thaler's mental accounting research describes how people divide money into separate, non-fungible mental categories, treating a salary differently from a windfall or a bonus [E43, E44]. This is directly relevant to the salary-day lever's design: money that has just arrived as salary sits in a mental account the customer associates with routine spending and bills, not with "investing," which is why the lever frames the ETF instalment as something set up alongside the salary credit, in the same moment the customer is already handling that money, rather than as a separate decision made from a different mental account later.

### 8.4 Present bias, defaults, and the SMarT precedent, with the joiners-only nuance

Madrian and Shea's 2001 study of automatic 401(k) enrollment finds that switching a US employer's retirement plan from an opt-in to an automatic default raised participation sharply, and that the effect was strongest among employees who are young, lower-paid, or Black or Hispanic, the groups whose opt-in participation had been lowest [E07, E11]. A secondary summary of the same study reports a 48 percentage-point rise in participation among newly hired employees under the new default, and an 11 percentage-point rise overall, about 15 months after the change [E09]. Carroll, Choi, Laibson, Madrian and Metrick's follow-up work on "active decisions" finds that requiring a new hire to make a choice, rather than defaulting them in or out, raised the initial enrollment fraction by 28 percentage points relative to standard opt-in enrollment, and that the savings-rate distribution reached three months after an active-decision hire would otherwise take three years to reach under standard opt-in design [E13, E14, E15]. The same research notes that a well-chosen default, rather than a forced active decision, is the better design specifically where financial illiteracy is high [E16], a relevant caution for a segment where, as section 8.6 below shows, measured financial-market literacy in India is not high.

**The joiners-only nuance:** Thaler and Benartzi's 2004 Save More Tomorrow (SMarT) programme is the most frequently cited behavioural precedent for this kind of design; among employees at one manufacturer who actively joined SMarT, average savings rates rose from 3.5% of income before joining to 13.6% by the fourth pay rise after joining [E03]. **That 3.5%-to-13.6% figure describes only the employees who chose to join the plan.** Across the whole employee base offered the plan at the same manufacturer, including those who never joined, average savings rose from a smaller 4.4% to 10.6% [E04]. SMarT has since been adopted by more than half of large US retirement plans and is incorporated into the US Pension Protection Act of 2006 [E06]. ONL's own model mirrors this distinction directly: lever A21, the tap-through rate on the salary-day card, and A22, the first-order rate after a tap, both describe the subset of customers who engage with the card, not the whole 6,27,853-account Salary Account cohort [T1, C-E2a]. Citing SMarT's headline number as if it applied to every salary customer, rather than to the smaller group who actively engage, would repeat the exact framing error the joiners-only nuance corrects.

Table 8.1a states the joiners-only nuance as a direct side-by-side comparison, since it is the single most frequently miscited figure in this literature.

| Population | Average savings rate before SMarT | Average savings rate by the fourth pay rise after joining | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|

### 8.5 Reminders and the fresh-start effect

Karlan, McConnell, Mullainathan and Zinman's field experiments across banks in Bolivia, Peru and the Philippines test whether reminder messages raise attainment of self-set savings goals among people who had recently opened a commitment savings account [E17, E18]. Messages naming both the specific savings goal and a financial incentive performed better than messages that varied only gain or loss framing, which showed no significant difference between frames [E19]; sending additional late reminders beyond the first did not add further lift [E20]. Milkman, Beshears, Choi, Laibson and Madrian's study of flu-vaccination reminders at one large US firm found that a plain reminder produced a 33.1% vaccination rate in the control group, a prompt to write a planned date (without a time) raised this by 1.5 percentage points, not statistically significant, while a prompt naming a specific planned date and time raised it by a statistically significant 4.2 percentage points [E27 to E29]. The specificity of the moment named in a reminder, not the reminder's mere existence, is the finding that carries across to ONL's lever design: the holdings-check lever (section 13) and the salary-day card both name a specific, recurring moment (the salary credit date) rather than sending a generic "don't forget your account" message.

Dai, Milkman and Riis's fresh-start research finds aspirational behaviour, measured through diet-related search volume, gym visits and self-set goal commitments, rises after temporal landmarks. New Year is one such landmark; birthdays, the start of a week, the start of a month, and the start of a school term produce the same rise [E22, E23, E24]. The proposed mechanism is that a landmark creates psychological separation between a person's past, including past inaction, and a new period, raising motivation to act in that new period [E25]. The 13-week programme window in this plan (section 26) deliberately opens inside this logic, beginning 2 November 2026, close to Dhanteras and the run-up to the new financial quarter, and the salary-day lever repeats a fresh-start-style landmark every single month, at every salary credit, rather than relying on the calendar's one annual new year.

### 8.7 The line between a nudge and a dark pattern

Table 8.2 draws the line this document's own copy rules (section 19) are built to respect, using the CCPA's own categories (section 7.15) as the test.

| Design choice | A nudge (used in this plan's levers) | A dark pattern (ruled out) | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timing | message sent at a moment the customer is already engaged (salary credit, a statement date) | message timed to create false urgency ("only today," "last chance") | [D75, C-K6] |
| Default | no default product selected; the customer picks from a fixed list or picks nothing | a pre-selected default the customer must notice and actively decline | [D75] |
| Framing | states what the account is and what an instalment is | frames inaction as a loss, or frames the account as about to disappear | [D75, C-K6] |
| Reward | no reward tied to completing a trade | a badge, streak, confetti or prize tied to trading frequency | [H35, H40, H41] |
| Exit | the customer can pause or stop an instalment at any time, in the same number of steps it took to start | cancellation requires more steps than signup, or is hidden | [D75] |

A nudge, in the sense this plan uses the word, changes when and how a fact is presented; it does not change what the customer is free to decide, and it carries no reward for deciding one way over another. Every row on the dark-pattern side of this table is the specific design Massachusetts and the FCA findings in section 6.3 already show raising trading frequency and risk-taking without the customer necessarily choosing that outcome on the merits [H35, H40, H41].
