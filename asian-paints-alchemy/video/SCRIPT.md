## S01 (cover and verdict, 38 words, about 14 s)
I'm Harsh Kawatra from Team IdeaForge, Delhi Technological University. Our answer to Case Study 1 fits in one
line: keep the rutile, hold it in a network of air, and seal the air in with the second coat.

## S02 (the challenge, 43 words, about 16 s)
Titanium dioxide has led whiteness for a century, but packed inside a liquid binder it scatters light far less
than it would in air. A premium white paint needs 40.8 grams of that pigment per square metre, two coats, to
look that bright.

## S03 (the obvious answers, rejected, 33 words, about 12 s)
We ran twelve white film designs through one optical model: new pigments, hollow particles, barium sulfate.
None beat rutile on brightness, and every particle design cost more film than the TiO2 it saved.

## S04 (the mechanism, 62 words, about 23 s)
Our design keeps the rutile, but moves the air out of the particle and into the film itself. A porous first
coat holds the pigment in thirty-five percent air; a sixteen point four micron seal coat keeps that air sealed
in without filling the pores. It borrows the same trick a Cyphochilus beetle scale uses to turn air into
brilliant white with no pigment.

## S05 (the moneyshot, 52 words, about 19 s)
That cuts TiO2 to 24.8 grams a square metre, 39% less, at the same contrast ratio and brightness as the
premium paint. At 40% adoption it saves about 298 crore rupees a year in materials and keeps 7,551 tonnes of
TiO2 out of the mix. Even our bear case holds at 15%.

## S06 (delivery, pulled back, 40 words, about 15 s)

two coats. Lab panels land by month six, an interior pilot by month twelve, each checked against a published
pass mark.
