# Rhetorical Engineering

The patterns in `structure.md` and `archetypes.md` describe *what* a winning deck contains. This document describes *how the deck persuades*. A deck with the right structure but the wrong rhetoric loses. A deck with sharp rhetoric and slightly imperfect structure can still win.

These tactics are observed across 3/3 corpus winners (GCCH M&A decks) and are likely to generalize to any persuasive analytical deck — they reflect principles of analytical writing that apply across case types.

---

## 1. The contrarian recommendation, engineered

A winning deck typically recommends *against* the obvious answer suggested by the case prompt and *for* a less-obvious alternative.

**Why this works:**
1. It demonstrates more critical thinking than defending the obvious answer
2. The alternative is a contribution beyond the case prompt — the team did the case *and* its meta-question
3. The contrarian answer is harder to defend, so doing it well signals stronger work

**How to construct it:**

**Step 1: Identify the actor's strategic gap.** What does the actor *need* that they don't have? Examples:

| Case type | The actor's gap |
|---|---|
| M&A (Cash Cows 2019) | GM needs an EV pickup (core customers are pickup buyers; their EV lineup was sedans/SUVs) |
| M&A (Providentia 2020) | Amazon needs proprietary content (Prime Video lacks the quality library to win streaming wars) |
| M&A (Kicking A$$ets 2021) | Nvidia needs AI/5G/edge architectures (GPUs losing share to ASICs in AI training) |
| Strategy | Company needs growth but is constrained by saturation in core market |
| Marketing | Brand needs to refresh appeal without losing core equity |
| Social impact | Funder needs scale + sustainability; current programs hit a ceiling |
| Policy | Country needs leverage without escalation that hurts itself |
| Operations | Firm needs throughput improvement without massive capex |

**Step 2: Note that the obvious answer doesn't fully fill the gap.** This is the negative side of the case. The obvious answer addresses the symptom but not the cause, or partially addresses the cause but creates new problems.

**Step 3: Find a less-obvious answer that *does* fill the gap.** Often something smaller, more specific, more recently-emerged, or in an adjacent space.

| Case type | Less-obvious alternative |
|---|---|
| M&A | A smaller, more strategically-aligned acquisition target |
| Strategy | A specific segment or geography the company has overlooked |
| Marketing | A segment the brand has dismissed or under-served |
| Social impact | An intervention type the funder hasn't tried (institution-building vs aid; behavior change vs structure) |
| Policy | A different policy lever (targeted sanctions vs tariffs; coalition vs unilateral; negotiation vs threat) |
| Operations | A different redesign approach (distributed vs centralized; platform vs vertical) |

**Step 4: Construct three reasons each side.** Each reason should be quantitative or specific.

| Side | Reason 1 (quantitative) | Reason 2 (strategic fit) | Reason 3 (feasibility) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Don't [obvious] | Number-based critique | Strategic mismatch | Cannot execute |
| Do [alternative] | Number-based case | Strategic alignment | Can execute |

**Step 5: Test the recommendation.** Is the alternative:
- Feasible for the actor (financially, operationally, politically)?
- Specifically aligned with the strategic gap?
- Identifiable in advance from the context analysis (i.e., can you foreshadow it)?

If yes to all three: that's your recommendation.

## 2. Foreshadowing — the most powerful pattern in the corpus

All three winners seed the alternative thesis in the industry/market analysis section, 30+ slides before the alternative is named.

**The technique:**

In the industry analysis section, end on a slide that *names the specific gap* the alternative target fills, without naming the alternative. The reader is led to discover the recommendation themselves.

**Examples:**

**Cash Cows slide 14 (alternative named on slide 58):** *"Major car manufacturers are readying their product portfolios for a soon-to-be-the-present future, in which their core customers want their favorite cars to be EVs."* The slide shows EV pickup truck models from various makers. Punchline: *"Limited availability of EV models in the growing and highly profitable pickup truck segment represents a huge market opportunity."*

The reader sees: there's a gap in EV pickups. They don't yet know Rivian exists. Forty-four slides later, when Rivian is proposed, the reader thinks "Of course."

**Providentia slide 19 (alternative named on slide 64):** *"Amazon's Past Acquisitions"* — categorizes Amazon's M&A history. Punchline: *"Amazon has generally not participated in horizontal acquisitions ie acquisitions that inorganically increase the market share of the acquirer in a particular market."*

The reader sees: Amazon doesn't do horizontal deals. Netflix is horizontal. So Netflix doesn't fit Amazon's acquisition style. Forty-five slides later, when ViacomCBS (which is *vertical* for Amazon — adds content, complements distribution) is proposed, the reader thinks "Of course."

**Kicking A$$ets slide 12 (alternative named on slide 63):** *"AI Industry Implications"* — shows predicted change in data center architecture. Punchline: *"GPUs are used for almost all training. They will soon lose market share to ASICs."*

The reader sees: GPUs are declining in AI. Nvidia and AMD are both GPU companies. Merging them doubles down on a declining category. Fifty-one slides later, when MediaTek (which makes SoCs with ASIC-like architectures) is proposed, the reader thinks "Of course."

**How to construct your foreshadow:**

1. Identify the *gap* your alternative fills.
2. Find data showing the gap is real and growing (industry trend, customer preference, competitive position).
3. Build a slide late in the industry section that *names the gap explicitly*.
4. Do not mention the alternative target yet.
5. When the alternative is finally proposed, it should feel inevitable to the reader.

## 3. Symmetric treatment

When comparing two or more things — companies, financing options, acquisition paths, scenarios — use identical slide structures so the reader pattern-matches effortlessly.

**Examples from the corpus:**

- GM and Tesla each get the same 4–5 slides in the same order: Overview, Performance, EV/AV Response, Strengths, Challenges
- Cash deal infeasibility analysis vs Merger deal infeasibility analysis use parallel column structures
- Tesla's valuation and Rivian's valuation use the same football field template
- All four EV pickup truck competitors are evaluated on the same axes

**Why it works:** Pattern recognition is cognitively cheap. When slides repeat structure, the reader's attention frees up to engage with the content rather than re-orient to layout. They process more substance per slide.

**How to implement:** When you have multiple things to compare, decide the comparison template once and apply it to all. Don't customize per item.

**Mistakes to avoid:**
- Different slide structures for the acquirer vs the target (judges can't compare)
- Different chart types for the same metric across items (apples to oranges)
- Different number of bullets per item (creates a false impression of weight differences)

## 4. Premium decomposition

Never present "the premium" as a single number. Decompose into base premium + company-specific premium.

## 4. Decomposing the "premium" / "cost" / "uplift"

Never present a single gap-closing number. Decompose it into a base component + special component(s).

**Why it works:** A single number looks like napkin math. A decomposed number with specific reasons for each component looks rigorous and shows the team thought through why the gap exists.

**This tactic applies to any case type — the gap might be a premium, a cost-to-execute, an opportunity foregone, or an investment required. The decomposition pattern is the same.**

**Case-type variations:**

| Case type | Base component | Special component(s) |
|---|---|---|
| M&A | Industry M&A average premium (~30–55%) | Control premium / Poison Pill / founder voting / strategic IP (5–15%) |
| Strategy | Industry-average investment to enter | Capability-build premium / competitive-response cost / time-to-impact discount |
| Marketing | Industry CAC benchmark | Brand-stretch tax / channel-learning cost / measurement uncertainty |
| Social impact | Direct cost per beneficiary | Institutional overhead / sustainability investment / spillover effects |
| Policy | Direct welfare impact estimate | Retaliation discount / diplomatic cost / unintended consequences |
| Operations | Direct cost saving from redesign | Change cost / capability investment / supplier transition |

**Industry M&A premium ranges (for M&A cases):**
- Industrials: 35–45%
- Tech/Software: 35–55%
- Media/Entertainment: 40–55%
- Semiconductors: 40–50%
- Pharma: 50–80%
- Banking/Finance: 25–40%

Cite the source (Deloitte M&A reports, Reuters M&A data, comparable transactions in the sector).

**Examples of "special component" reasons (M&A):**
- 10% Elon Musk premium (Cash Cows 2019): Musk holds 20% of Tesla, shareholders attached to his vision
- 10% Poison Pill premium (Providentia 2020): Netflix's anti-takeover provisions adopted vs Icahn

**Other valid bases for company-specific premium / case-specific component:**
- Founder voting power (dual-class shares, supermajority requirements)
- Concentrated institutional ownership
- Strong brand or unique IP not captured in financials
- Anti-takeover provisions in corporate charter
- Capability gap (for non-M&A cases)
- Implementation friction (for operations / social impact)
- Coalition complexity (for policy)

**Even if you don't have an obvious special-component reason, find one.** A decomposed gap of "35% industry baseline + 5% [specific reason]" is more rigorous than a unified 40%.

## 5. Three-scenario thinking

Never give a single point estimate for an uncertain number. Always give three scenarios with named assumptions.

**Standard naming options:**
- Pessimistic / Realistic / Optimistic (Cash Cows)
- Grey Sky / Realistic / Blue Sky (Providentia)
- Bear / Base / Bull (Kicking A$$ets)

Pick one set per deck and stick with it.

**Each scenario needs explicit assumptions:**
- Production capacity or volume growth (e.g. "+16.1% CAGR")
- Pricing assumption (e.g. "-1.9% p.a.")
- Margin assumption (e.g. "9% target")

Show a small assumption box per scenario beneath the scenario chart.

**Common error:** Showing three lines on a chart without named assumptions for each. The lines don't mean anything unless the reader knows what differs between them.

**Where to use three-scenario thinking** (any case type):
- M&A: FCF projection (DCF input), revenue projection, synergy realization timeline
- Strategy: market share capture, capability ramp-up, competitive response
- Marketing: adoption rate, retention, ARPU progression
- Social impact: program reach, cost per outcome, scale-up speed
- Policy: compliance rate, retaliation severity, economic spillover
- Operations: throughput uplift, implementation speed, change cost

## 6. Pre-empting your own weaknesses

When using a methodology with known limits, acknowledge the limits inline rather than letting a judge raise them.

**Examples from corpus:**

- *"Multiples-based valuation should have a smaller weight than the DCF in assessing X's value"* — said *before* the multiples are presented in detail, capping the team's own claim before someone else does
- *"These precedent multiples aren't accurate measures to determine Netflix's EV because: 1. The targets didn't operate at the same scale… 2. The targets are content producers/distributors, but rarely both…"* — pre-emptive disclaimer
- *"Comparables have been given a less weightage (30%) because as mentioned before, Netflix's position in the industry is unique and it doesn't have a perfect substitute"* — explicit reasoning shown

**How to implement:**

When introducing any methodology that isn't airtight:
1. Use it
2. Quantify a result
3. Explicitly acknowledge what the methodology doesn't account for
4. Downweight the result in the final synthesis

This is a sophistication signal. Judges trust teams who acknowledge their methodology's limits more than teams who present every result with equal confidence.

## 7. Third-party validation

The deep dive on the chosen alternative target should include 2–4 quotes from named third parties (analysts, journalists, industry publications).

**Why it works:** Positions the recommendation as something the market already sees, not just the team's opinion. Reduces the contrarian risk — "this isn't just our take, here's what Morgan Stanley said."

**Examples (Cash Cows slide 59 — Rivian deep dive):**
- *"If Rivian can deliver on its promise… then the EV market will surely gain itself a whole new sector"* — Wired
- *"Tesla's domination of the electric vehicle market is unsustainable and will soon be disrupted by start-up Rivian"* — Adam Jonas, Morgan Stanley Analyst
- *"I am stunned by the specs here. We are talking about a best-case scenario for an all-electric pickup truck"* — Fred Lambert, Editor in Chief, Electrek

**Sources by case type:**
- M&A: equity analysts, industry journalists, named executives
- Strategy: industry analysts, consulting firms, academic experts in the sector
- Marketing: brand consultants, market researchers, named customer testimonials
- Social impact: program evaluators, beneficiary representatives, academic experts in the field
- Policy: foreign policy think tanks, academic experts, former officials
- Operations: industry analysts, technology assessors, named customer/supplier feedback

**Quality criteria for quotes:**
- Named source (with title and affiliation)
- Specific claim about the alternative (not the industry in general)
- Recent (within 12–18 months of the case deadline)
- From a credible publication or institution

**Mistakes to avoid:**
- Anonymous "industry expert" quotes
- Quotes from the alternative's own marketing materials
- Quotes that praise the broader space but not the specific alternative
- Old quotes (2+ years) without an update

## 8. Defensive depth

Every quantitative claim in the main deck should have a backup slide in the appendix.

**Why it works:**
- The main deck stays clean (key arguments only, no digressive evidence)
- Judges who skim see a tight argument; judges who probe find the receipts
- "Deep dive on following slide" or "More detail in appendix" footers signal "we've thought through this"

**How to implement:**

Build the appendix as you build the main deck. For each main-deck slide that makes a quantitative claim, ask:
- Where's the data behind this number?
- What sensitivity analysis supports this?
- What's the methodology in detail?

If the answer requires more than a footnote, build an appendix slide.

**Appendix slide types:**
- Full financial model (year-by-year line items, often not in main deck)
- Peer group exclusion rationale (why the rejected peer groups don't fit)
- Sub-segment analyses
- Sensitivity matrices
- Methodology disclosures
- Source-detail tables (especially when a chart aggregates many sources)

The appendix should be referenced from the main deck via footers like *"Deep dive on following slide"* or *"More detail in appendix, p. X."*

## 9. The double synthesis

End the case-against section with one synthesis slide ("Conclusively, X should not acquire Y"). End the whole deck with another synthesis slide ("Therefore, X should acquire Z"). Two distinct payoff moments.

**Why it works:**
- First synthesis locks in the negative verdict before the deck pivots to the alternative
- Second synthesis commits to the positive alternative
- Two payoffs reinforce each other — judges remember "should not" AND "should instead"

**First synthesis (Acquisition Feasibility section closer):**
- Headline: the final negative recommendation
- Body: three columns synthesizing the case-against
  - Column 1: The value destruction
  - Column 2: The feasibility issues
  - Column 3: The verdict ("X should NOT acquire Y")

**Second synthesis (Conclusion):**
- Headline: the final positive recommendation
- Body: Q&A grid or three-card or section-by-section recap
- No new content

**Cash Cows did this:**
- Slide 54: "General Motors should not acquire Tesla" (three columns: value destroyed, challenges, verdict)
- Slide 63: "GM should not acquire Tesla, but instead acquire Rivian…" (Q&A grid)

**Kicking A$$ets did this:**
- Slide 58: "Nvidia should NOT acquire Advanced Micro Devices" ("Conclusively…")
- Slide 72: "Nvidia Should Not Acquire AMD and Should Instead Opt for…MediaTek" ("Therefore…")

## 10. Action titles, never topic titles

Every content slide's headline should be a full-sentence finding with a number where possible.

**Strong examples:**
- "More than 50 percent of the automotive industry's revenue will be in disrupted business areas by 2030"
- "Trading Multiples suggest Netflix is highly overvalued"
- "We forecast Tesla's cumulative free cash flow to differ from USD 24bn to 82bn in a scenario analysis primarily based on vehicle production and prices as well as margins"
- "Nvidia has gone through 17 total acquisitions. Most are financed in late stage."

**Weak examples (avoid):**
- "Industry Trends" → → "EV adoption is accelerating faster than analysts predicted, with 28% of new car sales projected to be electric by 2030"
- "Amazon's Past Acquisitions" → → "Amazon has acquired 17 companies in the last decade, only 1 of which was a horizontal acquisition — implying Amazon doesn't typically buy direct competitors"
- "Strategic Fit Analysis" → → "Only 3 of 6 strategic fit dimensions show meaningful overlap between GM and Tesla"

**How to test:** Read the title aloud. Can someone who's never seen the slide know what the slide concludes from just the title? If no, rewrite.

## 11. Quantification at every opportunity

Numbers are persuasion currency in case competition decks. Every claim should have a number where possible.

**Strong:**
- "Tesla is overvalued by 18% based on DCF and multiples analysis"
- "GM has 25.4% share of the US pickup truck market"
- "Industry M&A premium averages 37.5% over the last 5 years"

**Weak:**
- "Tesla appears to be overvalued"
- "GM has significant share of the US pickup market"
- "M&A premium is typically high"

**When you can't get a number, find a quantitative comparison:** "X is twice as large as Y", "the largest such deal in 5 years", "the only Y where Z is true."

## 12. The visual climax — make the moneyshot huge

The "$X bn destroyed" slide is the deck's emotional and visual climax. The whole financial section builds to it.

**Visual rules for the moneyshot:**
1. The destroyed-value number should be the largest text on the slide
2. Center it or place it as the visual focal point
3. Use a strong color (red or orange) for the number
4. Keep the surrounding waterfall clean — no clutter around the number
5. Repeat the number once more on the section closer slide that follows

**Slide grammar for the moneyshot:**
- Top: action title (e.g. "GM would lose around USD 26.2bn on the acquisition after accounting for a premium")
- Body: waterfall comparing built-up value to built-up purchase price
- Bottom: "Estimated loss on an investment in [Target] equals USD $X.X bn" in large bold

## 13. The Conclusion is recap, not new content

The conclusion synthesis slide must contain only content that has appeared earlier in the deck. No new arguments. No new numbers. No introduction of new concepts.

**Why this matters:**
- A conclusion that introduces new content suggests the team had spare arguments and didn't structure the deck tightly
- A conclusion that's pure recap signals discipline — every previous slide was load-bearing for the conclusion

**Test:** For each bullet on the conclusion slide, can you point to a specific earlier slide that supports it? If no, that bullet is new content. Remove it or move it earlier.

---

## 14. Citation discipline

Harvard-style references are required by the case rules. But the quality of citation discipline still varies and is a sophistication signal.

**On every content slide:**
- Source line at the bottom in small type
- Format: "Source: [Author, Year, Publication]" or for charts, list the data sources
- Multiple sources separated by commas or semicolons

**In the bibliography (final 5–13 slides):**
- Harvard-style format: *Author, Initial. (Year) Title. Publication [online]. Available at: URL (Accessed: Date)*
- Alphabetical by author surname
- Include every source cited anywhere in the deck
- Dense — 15–25 references per slide

**Common errors:**
- Missing sources on charts (judges notice)
- Sources without specific publication (just "internal estimate" or "team analysis" for too many claims)
- Inconsistent citation format
- Bibliography missing sources cited in the main deck

## 15. Avoid these failure modes

These appear even in winning decks. Avoid them when possible.

**Polish failures (specific to template-based decks):**
- Copy-paste artifacts (e.g., a line about GM/EVs in a Netflix deck — yes, this happened)
- Placeholder text ("Insert Text Here", "TODO", "xyz")
- Cross-deck contamination (using a chart from a different deck)

**Search before submitting:** Every deck should be searched for "TODO", "Insert", "Placeholder", "TBD", "xyz", "Lorem", company names from previous decks you might have referenced as templates.

**Structural failures:**
- Reframing the case question without acknowledging the reframe
- Missing the foreshadow (alternative arrives as a surprise)
- No section-closer slide before transitioning to alternative
- New content on the conclusion slide
- Single-number premium (no decomposition)
- Point estimate for an uncertain number (no scenarios)

**Visual failures:**
- 4+ charts on a single slide (over-crowding)
- No chart annotations
- Inconsistent color usage across slides
- Action titles that are descriptive (X is doing Y) instead of declarative (X is 18% overvalued)
- Missing sub-section labels at slide corners

**Rhetorical failures:**
- Generic third-party validation (anonymous "industry experts")
- Hedge language on the recommendation ("could potentially be considered" instead of "should not acquire")
- Bullets without specifics
- Symmetric structure abandoned mid-section
