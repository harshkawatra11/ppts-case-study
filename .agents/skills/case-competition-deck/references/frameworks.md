# Consulting Frameworks Catalog

A working reference of 40+ consulting frameworks, organized by purpose. Use this when:
- You need to pick the right framework for a specific case question
- You want to apply a known framework rigorously
- You want to layer multiple frameworks for deeper analysis
- You want to find lesser-known frameworks that give an edge over commodity SWOT/PESTLE thinking

**How to use this catalog:**
1. Identify what you're trying to do (analyze industry? evaluate options? design org? solve a problem?)
2. Use the selection guide below to pick frameworks
3. Read the framework's section for structure, application steps, examples, and common mistakes
4. Layer 2–3 frameworks where one would be too narrow (e.g., PESTLE + Porter's 5 Forces + Wardley Map for a deep external analysis)

**Important principle:** Frameworks are scaffolding for thinking, not answers in themselves. A consultant who just regurgitates a framework loses to one who picks the *right* framework, applies it rigorously, and synthesizes the output. Don't shoe-horn frameworks where they don't fit. Don't run every framework on every problem.

## 1. Problem structuring

### MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive)

**Originator:** Barbara Minto (McKinsey, 1960s–70s)

**One-paragraph description:** A way of structuring information into categories that don't overlap (mutually exclusive) and cover all possibilities (collectively exhaustive). It's the foundational discipline of consulting thought and presentation.

**When to use:** Always. MECE applies to every breakdown of a problem, every option set, every list of causes. It's not a "framework you pick" — it's the standard for every breakdown you produce.

**How it works:** When listing items, ask two questions:
1. Mutually Exclusive — does anything overlap? If A includes some of B, they're not ME.
2. Collectively Exhaustive — is anything missing? If the categories don't cover all possibilities, add a "Other" or another category.

**Example:** Decomposing "ways to reduce cost" — bad: ("labor cost", "people cost", "external spend", "supplier cost") = overlap and gaps. Good: ("direct labor", "indirect labor", "materials", "overhead") = MECE.

**Common mistakes:** Categories that overlap; missing a residual catch-all; categories at different levels of specificity.

---

### Issue trees

**One-paragraph description:** A tree-shaped MECE decomposition of a problem from the top question down to specific sub-questions or hypotheses that can be answered with data.

**When to use:** At the start of any case or project. The issue tree is the work plan.

**How it works:** Start with the core question at the top. Branch into 2–4 MECE sub-questions. Each sub-question branches into 2–4 sub-sub-questions. Stop when each leaf is a question you can answer with a specific analysis or piece of data.

**Example:** "How can [retailer] increase profit?" → ("Increase revenue") + ("Reduce cost") → each branches into ("Increase price", "Increase volume", "Add new revenue lines") and ("Reduce COGS", "Reduce SG&A", "Reduce capital cost").

**Two flavors:**
- **Question tree** — leaves are open questions (use for problem exploration)
- **Hypothesis tree** — leaves are testable hypotheses with yes/no answers (use when you have a working theory)

**Common mistakes:** Not MECE at each level; branches at different levels of specificity; too many levels (more than 4 is hard to manage).

### Pyramid Principle

**Originator:** Barbara Minto

**One-paragraph description:** A way of structuring written or spoken communication where you state the answer first (top of pyramid), then group supporting arguments (middle layer), then evidence and data (bottom layer). The opposite of how analysis flows; the way executive audiences consume.

**When to use:** When writing any executive communication — slides, memos, presentations, recommendations. Especially when delivering bad news or making a contrarian recommendation.

**How it works:**
1. Lead with the answer / recommendation (the "governing thought")
2. Support with 2–4 grouped reasons (each group MECE)
3. Under each reason, provide data and evidence

The structure should pass two tests:
- **Why test:** For each statement, asking "why?" should be answered by the next level down
- **So-what test:** For each statement, asking "so what?" should be answered by the level up

**Example:** Top: "X should not acquire Y." Middle: ("Y is overvalued by 18%", "Synergies don't justify premium", "Deal not financially feasible"). Bottom: data and analysis under each reason.

**Common mistakes:** Building the pyramid from data up (you'll only find what you went looking for); not testing the why/so-what chain; ungrouped supporting points at the same level.

---

### Hypothesis-driven approach

**One-paragraph description:** Start with a testable answer ("hypothesis"), then design analyses to confirm or disprove it. Opposite of "boil the ocean" — gather all data, then analyze.

**When to use:** Always (mature consultants are always hypothesis-driven). Especially when time / data is limited.

**How it works:**
1. Form an initial hypothesis based on prior knowledge and quick observation
2. Identify what data / analysis would confirm or refute it
3. Run those specific analyses
4. Update the hypothesis based on findings
5. Iterate until the hypothesis is confirmed, refuted, or sharpened

**Why it works:** Forces you to think before you analyze. Prioritizes the analyses that matter. Makes you faster.

**Common mistakes:** Confirmation bias (only looking at evidence that supports the hypothesis); not updating when evidence contradicts; over-committing to the initial hypothesis.

---

### So-what test

**One-paragraph description:** After every chart, finding, or analysis, ask "so what?" — what does this mean for the decision? If you can't answer, the analysis is incomplete or doesn't belong in the deck.

**When to use:** Every slide. Every analysis. Every chart.

**Common mistakes:** Presenting analysis without implications. Headlines that describe the chart instead of the finding. "Industry trends" instead of "Industry is consolidating — top 5 went from 32% share to 58% in 6 years, suggesting acquirers should move before targets become more expensive."

## 2. External / macro analysis

### PESTLE (or PESTEL, PEST, STEEP, STEEPLE)

**One-paragraph description:** Six macro factors that shape any industry or market: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental. The original is PEST; PESTLE adds Legal + Environmental; STEEPLE adds Ethics.

**When to use:** Beginning of any market / industry / country analysis. Especially valuable when entering new geographies (where local PESTLE differs).

**How it works:** Build a table or 6-quadrant slide. For each factor, list 2–4 specific drivers affecting the case. Rate impact (high / medium / low) and time horizon (near-term / long-term).

**Example for entering a new country:**
- **Political** — Regime stability, regulatory unpredictability, government industrial policy
- **Economic** — GDP growth, interest rates, FX volatility, consumer purchasing power
- **Social** — Demographics, urbanization, attitudes toward foreign brands
- **Technological** — Internet penetration, mobile commerce maturity, digital infrastructure
- **Legal** — Foreign ownership restrictions, IP protection, employment law
- **Environmental** — Climate exposure, sustainability regulations, resource constraints

**Strength:** Forces consideration of factors outside the immediate competitive lens.

**Common mistakes:** Treating it as a checkbox exercise (listing without analyzing); not ranking impact; not connecting to specific implications for the case.

---

### Porter's Five Forces

**Originator:** Michael Porter (HBS, 1979)

**One-paragraph description:** Five forces that determine industry profitability: threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitute products, rivalry among existing competitors.

**When to use:** When analyzing industry attractiveness, competitive intensity, or considering market entry.

**How it works:** For each of the 5 forces, list the specific factors that drive it strong / weak. Rate the force as high / medium / low. Identify which force(s) most constrain or enable profitability.

**The forces and what drives each:**

| Force | Strong (bad for incumbents) when… |
|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | Low capital required, no economies of scale, weak brand loyalty, no regulatory barriers |
| Bargaining power of suppliers | Few suppliers, high switching costs to alternative suppliers, supplier-side concentration |
| Bargaining power of buyers | Few large buyers, low switching cost for them, undifferentiated products |
| Threat of substitutes | Substitutes cheaper or better, low switching cost, customers willing to switch |
| Rivalry among existing | Many similar-sized competitors, slow industry growth, high exit barriers, undifferentiated products |

**Example:** Soft drinks — direct rivalry is moderate (few major players), but substitutes are now high (energy drinks, sparkling water, functional beverages) — and that's the bigger threat than rivalry.

**Strength:** Forces consideration of indirect competitive pressures (substitutes, suppliers, buyers) often missed in head-to-head analyses.

**Common mistakes:** Defining industry too narrowly (then missing substitutes) or too broadly (then everything becomes weak); not specifying which force most constrains profitability; treating as a static snapshot (forces change).

### Porter's Diamond Model (national competitiveness)

**Originator:** Michael Porter (1990)

**One-paragraph description:** Four interlinked attributes that determine why some nations or regions develop globally competitive industries: factor conditions, demand conditions, related & supporting industries, firm strategy & rivalry. Plus two external factors: government and chance.

**When to use:** When analyzing why a country / region has a competitive advantage in a specific industry, or when picking which country to invest in for a sector.

**How it works:** For each diamond corner, assess strength in the target country/region:
- **Factor conditions** — Skilled labor, infrastructure, capital availability, natural resources
- **Demand conditions** — Sophisticated, demanding domestic buyers driving innovation
- **Related & supporting industries** — Strong supplier base, complementary industries
- **Firm strategy, structure, rivalry** — Domestic competition forcing efficiency

**Example:** Why Germany dominates premium auto — sophisticated domestic buyers, world-class engineering education, deep supplier ecosystem (Bosch, ZF, Continental), intense rivalry between Mercedes/BMW/Porsche/Audi.

**Common mistakes:** Treating each corner in isolation (the power is in their interaction); ignoring government / chance.

---

### Industry Life Cycle

**One-paragraph description:** Industries progress through stages: Introduction → Growth → Shakeout → Maturity → Decline. Strategy differs by stage.

**When to use:** When the case involves timing — when to enter, when to exit, what kind of strategy to pursue.

**Stages and implications:**

| Stage | Growth | Competitors | Profitability | Strategic focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Low/uncertain | Few pioneers | Low/negative | Build awareness, learn customer needs |
| Growth | High | Increasing entry | Improving | Capture share, scale |
| Shakeout | Slowing | Consolidation | Volatile | M&A, efficiency, differentiate |
| Maturity | Low | Few large players | High but stable | Defend share, efficiency, niche |
| Decline | Negative | Exit / consolidation | Falling | Harvest, divest, or last-man-standing |

**Common mistakes:** Misjudging the stage; assuming linear progression (some industries get disrupted before reaching maturity); not segmenting (different segments may be at different stages).

### Wardley Mapping

**Originator:** Simon Wardley (2005)

**One-paragraph description:** A strategy mapping technique that visualizes the *value chain* of a business on a Y-axis (visible to customer at top, invisible infrastructure at bottom) against the *evolution* of each component on an X-axis (genesis → custom → product → commodity). Reveals where to compete, where to outsource, where to commoditize.

**When to use:** When the case involves technology strategy, platform strategy, build-vs-buy decisions, or competitive response to digital disruption. Especially valuable for strategy in fast-evolving tech / digital contexts.

**How it works:**
1. List the components of your value chain, from customer-visible (top of Y-axis) to deep infrastructure (bottom of Y-axis)
2. For each component, position on evolution axis (X-axis): is it custom-built, packaged product, or commodity utility?
3. Identify where the components *will* move on the evolution axis over time
4. Strategy implications: invest in the genesis/custom components (where differentiation lives); outsource/commoditize the right side (where it's becoming utility)

**Example:** Cloud computing in 2008 — compute was custom (data centers); AWS moved it to commodity (utility). Companies that built strategies assuming compute would stay custom lost; companies that bet on compute commoditization won.

**Strength:** Captures *evolution over time* — most frameworks are static snapshots. Forces explicit thinking about what's becoming commoditized.

**Why it's underused:** Steeper learning curve than other frameworks. Worth the investment for tech / platform strategy.

**Common mistakes:** Confusing X-axis (evolution) with Y-axis (value chain); not iterating the map as you learn; treating components as static.

## 3. Internal analysis

### McKinsey 7-S Framework

**Originator:** McKinsey (Pascale & Athos, Peters & Waterman, late 1970s)

**One-paragraph description:** Seven interconnected elements that determine organizational effectiveness: Strategy, Structure, Systems, Shared values, Style, Staff, Skills. Three are "hard" (Strategy, Structure, Systems); four are "soft" (Shared values, Style, Staff, Skills).

**When to use:** When analyzing organizational alignment, planning a major change, assessing why a strategy isn't being executed, or doing post-merger integration.

**How it works:** Map current state of each S. Map desired future state. Identify gaps. Plan to close the gaps — recognizing that changing one S forces changes in others.

**The 7 S's:**
- **Strategy** — How the organization plans to win
- **Structure** — Reporting lines, hierarchy, geography
- **Systems** — Processes, IT, decision rights
- **Shared values** — Culture, the "why"
- **Style** — Leadership and management style
- **Staff** — People and talent capabilities
- **Skills** — Distinctive competencies

**Example (cited in the StrategyU article):** A tech company with high turnover thought it was a comp problem (Systems). 7-S analysis revealed misalignment between Strategy (innovative disruptor) and Shared values (risk-averse, hierarchical). Real fix: cultural change, not pay raise.

**Common mistakes:** Treating each S in isolation; addressing hard S's only (Strategy/Structure/Systems) and ignoring soft S's; expecting perfect alignment (dynamic environments make this impossible).

### Porter's Value Chain

**Originator:** Michael Porter (1985)

**One-paragraph description:** Decomposes a firm into 9 activities — 5 primary (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing & sales, service) and 4 support (procurement, technology, HR, infrastructure) — to identify where value is created and where competitive advantage can be built.

**When to use:** When analyzing where in a firm's operations the source of competitive advantage lies; when looking for cost reduction opportunities; when benchmarking against competitors.

**How it works:** For each of the 9 activities, ask:
- What value does this activity add for customers?
- What's the cost?
- Where do we have advantage / disadvantage vs competitors?
- Can this be optimized, outsourced, automated, or eliminated?

**Strength:** Forces granular, activity-level thinking instead of high-level "we have better operations."

**Common mistakes:** Treating the model as universal (some businesses are services with different activity structures); ignoring linkages between activities; not benchmarking.

### VRIO / VRIN (Resource-Based View)

**Originator:** Jay Barney (1991)

**One-paragraph description:** A test for whether a resource or capability creates *sustainable* competitive advantage. VRIO asks: is the resource Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, and Organized-to-capture-value? VRIN substitutes Non-substitutable for Organized.

**When to use:** When evaluating which capabilities to invest in / protect; when identifying real competitive advantages vs claimed ones; when assessing M&A target's value.

**How it works:** For each candidate resource or capability:
- **Valuable** — Does it allow the firm to exploit opportunities or neutralize threats?
- **Rare** — Few competitors have it
- **Inimitable** — Hard for competitors to copy or substitute (causal ambiguity, path dependence, social complexity)
- **Organized** — The firm is structured to actually capture the value

**Verdict ladder:**
- Not Valuable → competitive disadvantage
- Valuable + Not Rare → competitive parity
- Valuable + Rare + Imitable → temporary competitive advantage
- Valuable + Rare + Inimitable + Organized → sustainable competitive advantage

**Example:** Disney's IP library + theme park infrastructure + cross-property activation capability passes VRIO. Many companies have content libraries; Disney's combination is uniquely organized to monetize across formats.

**Common mistakes:** Claiming inimitability without specifying what blocks imitation; ignoring the "Organized" dimension (many firms have resources they can't actually exploit).

### Core Competencies (Hamel & Prahalad)

**Originator:** Gary Hamel & C.K. Prahalad (1990)

**One-paragraph description:** The few capabilities a firm has that (a) provide access to a wide variety of markets, (b) make a significant contribution to customer benefit, and (c) are difficult for competitors to imitate.

**When to use:** When deciding what to invest in, what to outsource, and which adjacent markets to enter.

**Three tests for a true core competency:**
1. Does it open access to multiple markets?
2. Does it deliver significant customer benefit?
3. Is it hard to imitate?

**Example:** Honda's core competency is engine design — it transfers across motorcycles, cars, lawnmowers, boats, generators. Multiple markets, real customer benefit, hard to imitate.

**Common mistakes:** Confusing a product with a core competency; listing too many ("a firm has a few core competencies, not twenty"); claiming uniqueness without rigor.

---

### BCG Capabilities Index

**One-paragraph description:** A BCG framework that goes deeper than core competencies — categorizes capabilities by source of advantage (scale economies, network effects, learning curves, etc.) and assesses each one's strength vs rivals.

**When to use:** When you want a more granular view than core competencies, especially for capability investment prioritization.

**How it works:** Inventory each capability. For each, identify the source of advantage. Rate strength vs each major competitor (Leader / Parity / Follower).

## 4. Strategic positioning

### Porter's Generic Strategies

**Originator:** Michael Porter (1980)

**One-paragraph description:** Three (or four) generic strategies for competitive advantage: Cost Leadership, Differentiation, and Focus (which splits into Cost Focus and Differentiation Focus). A firm must pick one or get "stuck in the middle."

**When to use:** When defining or stress-testing competitive positioning, especially during strategic planning or after a competitor's move.

**The strategies:**

| Strategy | Scope | Source of advantage | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost Leadership | Industry-wide | Lowest cost | Walmart, IKEA, Ryanair |
| Differentiation | Industry-wide | Unique value perception | Apple, BMW, Patek Philippe |
| Cost Focus | Niche | Lowest cost in niche | Aldi (within grocery) |
| Differentiation Focus | Niche | Unique value in niche | Tesla pre-2018, Ferrari |

**Stuck in the middle:** Firms that try to do both cost leadership and differentiation often fail at both. Sears was Porter's classic example.

**Modern critique:** Some firms (Toyota, Amazon) seem to achieve both. The defense: they segment so internally they're effectively differentiating *and* cost-leading in different segments.

**Common mistakes:** Claiming differentiation without specifying what makes you different; cost leadership without rigorous cost benchmarking; trying to compete on multiple dimensions simultaneously.

---

### Value Disciplines (Treacy & Wiersema)

**Originator:** Michael Treacy & Fred Wiersema (1993)

**One-paragraph description:** Three value disciplines that companies can lead on: Operational Excellence, Product Leadership, Customer Intimacy. Pick one to lead; meet "table stakes" on the other two.

**When to use:** When the firm is trying to clarify its market promise to customers and align operations behind it.

**The three disciplines:**

| Discipline | Promise to customer | Operating model focus |
|---|---|---|
| Operational Excellence | Best total cost, no hassle | Process discipline, scale, efficiency |
| Product Leadership | Best product, leading-edge | R&D, innovation, fast launch |
| Customer Intimacy | Tailored solution to your specific needs | CRM, relationships, customization |

**Examples:** Walmart (Operational), Apple (Product), Nordstrom (Customer Intimate).

**Common mistakes:** Trying to lead on all three (impossible operational tradeoffs); claiming Customer Intimacy without the infrastructure to deliver tailored solutions.

### Blue Ocean Strategy + Strategy Canvas + ERRC Grid

**Originator:** W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne (2005)

**One-paragraph description:** Move from competing in bloody "red oceans" (existing markets with intense competition) to creating uncontested "blue oceans" (new market space). The key tools are the Strategy Canvas (visualize current competition) and the Four Actions / ERRC Grid (Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create).

**When to use:** When the firm is locked in commodity competition; when entering an industry as a new player; when innovation cases ask "how do we break out of the current competitive dynamic?"

**How it works:**

**Strategy Canvas:**
- X-axis: factors the industry competes on (price, quality, service, brand, range, etc.)
- Y-axis: level of investment / performance on each factor
- Plot competitors as lines. Most look similar = red ocean.
- A blue ocean strategy has a *different* shape — high on some factors, low on others, and may add new factors.

**ERRC Grid (Four Actions Framework):** For each industry competitive factor, decide:
- **Eliminate** — Which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated?
- **Reduce** — Which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard?
- **Raise** — Which factors should be raised well above the industry standard?
- **Create** — Which new factors should be created that the industry has never offered?

**Example (Cirque du Soleil):**
- Eliminate: star performers, animal shows, multiple-show arenas
- Reduce: thrill/danger, fun/humor
- Raise: artistic environment, unique venue
- Create: theme, refined ambiance, multiple productions

**Related tools:**
- **Six Paths Framework** — six ways to look across the boundaries of current competition (across industries, strategic groups, buyer groups, complementary offerings, functional/emotional appeal, time)
- **Buyer Utility Map** — 6 stages of buyer experience × 6 utility levers to identify untapped value
- **Three Tiers of Non-customers** — Soon-to-be, refusing, unexplored — what blue ocean opens to them?

**Common mistakes:** Treating Blue Ocean as the *answer* (most strategies are red ocean execution, not blue ocean creation); confusing differentiation with blue ocean (blue ocean is about creating new demand, not just being different); using ERRC without first building the strategy canvas.

### Playing to Win (Lafley & Martin)

**Originator:** A.G. Lafley & Roger Martin (2013, Procter & Gamble)

**One-paragraph description:** Strategy is a set of integrated choices across five linked questions: What's our winning aspiration? Where will we play? How will we win? What capabilities must we have? What management systems do we need?

**When to use:** When developing or refreshing corporate strategy. Especially good at forcing integrated choices rather than scattered initiatives.

**The five questions:**
1. **Winning aspiration** — What does winning look like? (Not "be the best" — specific.)
2. **Where to play** — Which geographies, segments, products, channels, vertical stages?
3. **How to win** — On what basis will we win in chosen markets?
4. **Capabilities** — What must we be great at?
5. **Management systems** — What systems, structures, measures support the choices?

The five must be consistent. A choice at one level constrains the others.

**Strength:** Forces explicit *choice* (and exclusion). "We will not play in X" is as important as "we will play in Y."

**Common mistakes:** Listing aspirations and capabilities without making the actual choices; "where to play" too broad (loses focus); "how to win" without genuine basis (saying "better quality" without explaining why).

### Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers

**Originator:** Hamilton Helmer (2016)

**One-paragraph description:** A framework identifying the seven types of *sustainable* competitive advantage. The argument: advantage requires both a benefit (over rivals) and a barrier (preventing them from eroding it).

**When to use:** When evaluating whether a competitive advantage is sustainable — especially valuable for tech companies, platforms, and investing.

**The 7 Powers:**

| Power | Benefit | Barrier |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale Economies** | Lower per-unit cost | Larger competitor would lose at lower scale |
| **Network Economies** | Increasing value per user as network grows | Smaller competitor can't match value |
| **Counter-Positioning** | Better business model that incumbents can't adopt without cannibalizing themselves | Incumbent's existing business prevents adoption |
| **Switching Costs** | Customers locked in (data, integration, habit, contract) | Customers won't move even for better product |
| **Branding** | Customers pay more / are loyal due to brand affect | Reputation takes decades to build |
| **Cornered Resource** | Unique access to a critical input (IP, talent, raw material) | Resource not available to rivals |
| **Process Power** | Embedded operational excellence | Years of tacit learning, integration, alignment |

Each Power has a specific origin (the moment it can be acquired) and is sustained through its barrier.

**Example:** Netflix's early lead in streaming combined Scale Economies (more subscribers → more content investment), Counter-Positioning (cable companies couldn't pivot to streaming without cannibalizing existing revenue), and later Process Power (recommendation algorithm).

**Strength:** More rigorous than generic "moats" thinking. Forces explicit identification of the barrier, not just the benefit.

**Why it matters:** A "competitive advantage" without a clear barrier isn't sustainable. Many strategy decks claim moats they don't have.

**Common mistakes:** Confusing operational excellence with Process Power (Process Power requires it to be embedded across teams over years); claiming branding without evidence customers pay more; confusing network effects with scale effects.

## 5. Portfolio & growth

### BCG Growth-Share Matrix

**Originator:** Boston Consulting Group (Bruce Henderson, 1970)

**One-paragraph description:** A 2×2 portfolio matrix: relative market share (X) × market growth (Y). Plots business units / products into Stars, Cash Cows, Question Marks, Dogs to guide investment / divest decisions.

**When to use:** When managing a diversified portfolio of products / business units. Best for multi-business conglomerates and FMCG companies.

**The four quadrants:**

| Quadrant | Position | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| **Stars** | High growth, high share | Invest to maintain leadership |
| **Cash Cows** | Low growth, high share | Milk for cash; minimize investment |
| **Question Marks** | High growth, low share | Invest to grow (if can win) or divest |
| **Dogs** | Low growth, low share | Divest / harvest |

**How it works:**
- Use relative market share (your share / largest competitor's share) — 1.0 is the cutoff
- Use market growth rate — 10% is a common cutoff
- Size circles by revenue (or another metric)

**Strength:** Visual portfolio view; forces investment-rebalancing thinking.

**Critique:** Oversimplified (only 2 dimensions); market share isn't always the right success metric; growth/share isn't all that matters for investment decisions.

**Common mistakes:** Defining market too narrowly (everyone becomes a Star); using absolute share instead of relative; ignoring the dynamics — a Question Mark that doesn't move to Star becomes a Dog.

---

### GE / McKinsey 9-Box Matrix

**Originator:** GE + McKinsey (1970s)

**One-paragraph description:** A 3×3 matrix that extends BCG: Industry Attractiveness (Y) × Business Strength (X), with each axis having three levels. More nuanced than BCG's 2×2.

**When to use:** When BCG feels too crude. Especially for portfolios where market share isn't a great proxy for business strength.

**How it works:**
- Industry Attractiveness factors: market size, growth, profitability, competitive intensity, regulatory environment
- Business Strength factors: market share, brand strength, distribution, cost position, capabilities
- Score each unit on each axis (weighted average of factors)
- Place in 3×3 grid; investment strategy by cell

**Cell strategies:**

| | Attractiveness High | Attractiveness Med | Attractiveness Low |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strength High | **Invest / Grow** | Invest / Grow | Selectively invest |
| Strength Med | Invest / Grow | Selectively invest | Harvest |
| Strength Low | Selectively invest | Harvest | Divest |

**Common mistakes:** Subjective scoring (different analysts get different placements); not specifying the underlying factors; using only the diagonal cells (where the framework is most informative is in the off-diagonal cells where there's tension).

---

### Ansoff Matrix

**Originator:** Igor Ansoff (1957)

**One-paragraph description:** A 2×2 growth framework: existing/new markets × existing/new products. Four growth strategies with progressively higher risk: Market Penetration → Product Development → Market Development → Diversification.

**When to use:** When planning growth strategy. Forces a choice between four distinct growth paths with different risk profiles.

**The four strategies:**

| | Existing Products | New Products |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing Markets** | **Market Penetration** (lowest risk) — sell more to current customers | **Product Development** — develop new products for current customers |
| **New Markets** | **Market Development** — take current products to new markets | **Diversification** (highest risk) — new products to new markets |

**Diversification splits into:**
- Related diversification (leverages existing capabilities)
- Unrelated diversification (highest risk)

**Example (StrategyU article):** A grocery chain fixated on Market Penetration (squeezing more from existing stores). Ansoff thinking revealed Market Development (neighboring states) and Product Development (premium organic line) had more potential.

**Common mistakes:** Choosing only one strategy (the most successful companies pursue multiple); treating diversification as the answer (it's the riskiest); not assessing capability fit for the chosen path.

---

### Three Horizons (McKinsey)

**Originator:** McKinsey (Baghai, Coley, White, 1999)

**One-paragraph description:** Three time horizons for managing growth: Horizon 1 (extend & defend the core), Horizon 2 (build emerging businesses), Horizon 3 (create viable options for the future).

**When to use:** When the firm needs to balance short-term performance with future growth — especially when criticized for short-termism or for neglecting the core.

**The horizons:**

| Horizon | Timeframe | Focus | Metrics |
|---|---|---|---|
| **H1: Core** | 0–2 years | Extend & defend current business | Profitability, share, cash flow |
| **H2: Emerging** | 2–5 years | Build emerging high-growth businesses | Revenue growth, customer acquisition |
| **H3: Future** | 5+ years | Plant seeds for future options | Learning, capability building |

**Strength:** Prevents the trap where short-term performance crowds out future investment, or future bets crowd out the core.

**Common mistakes:** Treating horizons as sequential rather than simultaneous (all three should be active at once); under-investing in H3 (the easiest to defer); applying ROI metrics to H3 (premature — H3 is about optionality).

---

### Strategy Diamond (Hambrick & Fredrickson)

**Originator:** Donald Hambrick & James Fredrickson (2001)

**One-paragraph description:** Five elements that together define a strategy: Arenas (where), Vehicles (how to get there — build, buy, partner), Differentiators (how to win), Staging (sequencing), Economic Logic (how to make money).

**When to use:** As a comprehensiveness check on a strategy. If you can't fill in all five, the strategy is incomplete.

**The five:**
- **Arenas** — Where will we be active? Markets, segments, products, geographies, value chain stages.
- **Vehicles** — How will we get there? Organic growth, M&A, alliance, JV, licensing.
- **Differentiators** — How will we win? Brand, technology, cost, customer service, customization.
- **Staging** — At what speed and in what sequence?
- **Economic logic** — How will we obtain economic returns? Premium pricing, cost economies, network effects, scope economies.

**Strength:** More holistic than other frameworks — captures *how* (vehicles) and *when* (staging) which most strategy frameworks ignore.

**Common mistakes:** Defining arenas too broadly; not specifying differentiators with rigor; ignoring staging (assuming everything happens at once).

---

## 6. Customer & marketing

### 3 C's / 4 C's / 5 C's

**One-paragraph description:** Three (or four / five) lenses for situation analysis: Customer, Company, Competition, plus Collaborators (4 C's) plus Climate / Context (5 C's).

**When to use:** As a quick situation analysis at the start of any strategy or marketing case.

**The 5 C's:**
- **Company** — Capabilities, strategy, performance
- **Customers** — Segments, needs, behaviors, size
- **Competitors** — Direct and indirect, their positioning, threats
- **Collaborators** — Suppliers, distributors, partners, complementors
- **Climate** — Macro environment (essentially PESTLE)

**Common mistakes:** Treating it as a data dump (list everything you know); not synthesizing into implications.

---

### 4 P's of Marketing

**Originator:** E. Jerome McCarthy (1960), popularized by Kotler

**One-paragraph description:** The marketing mix: Product, Price, Place (distribution), Promotion. Each must be consistent with the others and with target customer.

**When to use:** When defining or evaluating a marketing strategy / tactical plan.

**The 4 P's:**
- **Product** — Features, quality, design, brand, packaging, services, warranty
- **Price** — List price, discounts, payment terms, allowances, financing
- **Place** — Channels, coverage, locations, inventory, logistics, e-commerce
- **Promotion** — Advertising, PR, sales promotion, personal selling, direct marketing

**Test:** Are all 4 P's aligned with the same target customer and positioning?

---

### 7 P's of Marketing (Services Marketing)

**Originator:** Booms & Bitner (1981)

**One-paragraph description:** Extends 4 P's for services: adds People, Process, Physical Evidence.

**When to use:** For service businesses (hotels, banks, consulting, healthcare, education).

**Additional P's:**
- **People** — Employee training, behavior, attitude — directly part of the service
- **Process** — Service delivery flow, wait times, automation
- **Physical Evidence** — Tangible cues that signal quality (lobby, uniform, documents, website)

---

### STP (Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning)

**One-paragraph description:** Three-step marketing strategy process: segment the market, target the most attractive segments, position the brand distinctively for those segments.

**When to use:** When designing marketing strategy from scratch or refreshing it.

**The three steps:**

1. **Segmentation** — Divide market into groups based on shared characteristics. Bases:
   - Demographic (age, income, gender)
   - Geographic (region, urban/rural)
   - Psychographic (values, lifestyle, personality)
   - Behavioral (usage, loyalty, occasion)
   - Needs-based (jobs to be done)

2. **Targeting** — Choose which segments to serve. Criteria:
   - Segment size and growth
   - Segment profitability
   - Competitive intensity in the segment
   - Fit with company capabilities

3. **Positioning** — Define a distinctive value proposition for each target segment. Position relative to competitors.

**Common mistakes:** Segmenting by easy-to-measure dimensions (demographics) instead of meaningful ones (needs); targeting too many segments (loses focus); positioning that could apply to any brand.

---

### AIDA (Marketing Funnel)

**One-paragraph description:** A four-stage funnel model of customer purchasing: Attention → Interest → Desire → Action.

**When to use:** When designing a campaign or analyzing where customers drop off in the marketing funnel.

**Variants:** AIDAS adds Satisfaction; AIDCAS adds Conviction. Modern digital funnels often use: Awareness → Consideration → Purchase → Loyalty → Advocacy.

---

### Pirate Metrics (AARRR)

**Originator:** Dave McClure (2007)

**One-paragraph description:** Five startup / growth metrics: Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, Revenue. Named "Pirate" because the letters spell "AARRR."

**When to use:** For digital products, subscription businesses, marketplaces, SaaS — anywhere user funnel optimization matters.

**The stages:**
- **Acquisition** — How do users find you?
- **Activation** — Do they have a good first experience?
- **Retention** — Do they come back?
- **Referral** — Do they tell others?
- **Revenue** — Do you make money?

**Application:** Build a funnel diagram. Measure conversion at each stage. Identify the largest leak and fix that first.

---

### Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)

**Originator:** Clayton Christensen (popularized 2003)

**One-paragraph description:** Customers don't buy products; they "hire" products to do a job for them. The job is the underlying need; the product is one of many ways to do that job.

**When to use:** When competing in a saturated category; when looking for innovation opportunities; when redefining competition.

**How it works:**
1. Identify the job — what is the customer ultimately trying to achieve?
2. Identify the alternatives — what else could do this job?
3. Identify the desired outcomes — how does the customer measure success of the job?
4. Find where current solutions fail to satisfy outcomes — that's the opportunity

**Christensen's example:** McDonald's discovered the morning milkshake customer was "hiring" the milkshake for a job — entertain me during my boring morning commute, keep me full until lunch. Competitors: bagel (messy), banana (eats too fast), candy bar (feels indulgent). The milkshake won because it took 20 minutes to drink. Reformulating around this job led to growth.

**Common mistakes:** Confusing the product with the job; defining the job too narrowly (functional only — missing emotional / social jobs).

---

### Kano Model

**Originator:** Noriaki Kano (1984)

**One-paragraph description:** A model classifying product attributes by their relationship to customer satisfaction: Must-haves (basic needs — present = neutral, absent = dissatisfied), Performance (linear — more = more satisfied), Delighters (absent = neutral, present = delighted).

**When to use:** When prioritizing features in product development; when allocating resources between meeting baseline expectations and creating distinctive value.

**The categories:**

| Type | Effect of presence | Effect of absence |
|---|---|---|
| **Must-have** | Customer takes for granted | Strong dissatisfaction |
| **Performance** | Satisfaction increases linearly | Dissatisfaction increases linearly |
| **Delighter** | Strong delight | Customer doesn't notice |
| Indifferent | No effect | No effect |
| Reverse | Dissatisfaction | Satisfaction |

**Time dynamic:** Today's delighters become tomorrow's must-haves (e.g., smartphone touchscreens).

**Common mistakes:** Investing in must-haves beyond basic competence (no additional satisfaction); investing in performance attributes without measuring trade-offs.

---

### Customer Journey Map

**One-paragraph description:** A visualization of every step a customer takes from need awareness through purchase to advocacy, with their actions, thoughts, emotions, and pain points at each step.

**When to use:** When designing or improving customer experience; when identifying friction points; when designing service blueprints.

**Standard elements:**
- Stages (Awareness → Consideration → Purchase → Use → Loyalty)
- Customer actions at each stage
- Touchpoints (where the customer interacts)
- Customer thoughts and emotions
- Pain points / moments of truth
- Opportunities

---

### Service Blueprint

**Originator:** G. Lynn Shostack (1984)

**One-paragraph description:** Extension of customer journey map to include the operational support behind each customer touchpoint, organized in swim lanes: customer actions, frontstage employee actions, backstage employee actions, support processes.

**When to use:** When redesigning a service (especially services with significant operational components — hospitality, healthcare, retail, professional services).

**The four swim lanes:**
- **Customer actions** — what the customer does
- **Frontstage** — visible employee actions (customer-facing)
- **Backstage** — invisible employee actions (kitchen, back office)
- **Support processes** — IT systems, suppliers, infrastructure

**Strength:** Surfaces the operational dependencies behind each customer moment.

---

### RFM Analysis (Recency, Frequency, Monetary)

**One-paragraph description:** Customer segmentation method based on three behavioral metrics: Recency of last purchase, Frequency of purchases, Monetary value of purchases. Each customer scored 1–5 on each dimension, yielding 125 segments collapsed to actionable groups.

**When to use:** For direct marketing, CRM, retail. When prioritizing customer marketing investment.

**Output segments:** Champions (high R, F, M), Loyal Customers (high F, M), Potential Loyalists (high R, M), At Risk (low R, high F/M), Lost (low R, F, M).

---

### Net Promoter Score (NPS)

**Originator:** Frederick Reichheld / Bain (2003)

**One-paragraph description:** A single metric (% Promoters minus % Detractors) measuring customer loyalty, based on "How likely are you to recommend us to a friend or colleague?" (0–10 scale).

**When to use:** As a continuous customer loyalty measurement; as a leading indicator of growth.

**Scoring:**
- 0–6 = Detractors
- 7–8 = Passives
- 9–10 = Promoters
- NPS = % Promoters − % Detractors

**Strength:** Single number, easy to track, correlates with revenue growth in many industries.

**Critique:** Often gamed; doesn't capture *why* customers feel the way they do.

---

## 7. Innovation

### Disruptive Innovation Theory (Christensen)

**Originator:** Clayton Christensen (1997, *The Innovator's Dilemma*)

**One-paragraph description:** A specific theory about how incumbents lose to new entrants who serve overlooked low-end or new-market segments with simpler, cheaper offerings — eventually moving upmarket and displacing the incumbent. "Disruption" is a precise term, not a synonym for "innovation."

**When to use:** When evaluating whether a new entrant is a real threat; when planning a response to a low-end competitor; when considering whether to disrupt yourself.

**Key principles:**
- Incumbents are excellent at sustaining innovation (improving products for existing customers)
- Incumbents are systematically *bad* at disruptive innovation because it would cannibalize their best customers
- Disruptors enter at low end (worse performance, lower price), then improve faster than the mainstream
- Eventually disruptors are "good enough" for mainstream — incumbents are left to retreat upmarket

**Two flavors:**
- **Low-end disruption** — Targets overserved low-end customers (Toyota's early entry in US)
- **New-market disruption** — Targets non-consumers (PCs disrupting mainframes)

**Common mistakes:** Using "disruption" loosely (most innovations are sustaining, not disruptive); panicking about every new entrant; treating disruption theory as a complete strategy (it's a diagnostic, not a prescription).

---

### Crossing the Chasm (Moore)

**Originator:** Geoffrey Moore (1991)

**One-paragraph description:** A modification of the technology adoption life cycle showing a "chasm" between early adopters (innovators + early adopters) and the early majority. Many tech products win early adopters but fail to cross to mainstream.

**When to use:** For technology / innovation cases — especially evaluating whether a product can reach mass market.

**The adoption curve:**
- Innovators (2.5%) — tech enthusiasts
- Early Adopters (13.5%) — visionaries
- **CHASM**
- Early Majority (34%) — pragmatists
- Late Majority (34%) — conservatives
- Laggards (16%)

**Crossing strategy:**
- Pick a *beachhead* — a specific niche in the early majority
- Become 100% solution for that niche (whole product concept)
- Dominate it
- Expand from there (bowling pin strategy)

**Why the chasm exists:** Early adopters want disruptive change. Early majority wants productivity improvements with low risk. Different needs require different go-to-market.

**Common mistakes:** Trying to be everything to everyone in the mainstream; abandoning the beachhead niche too early.

---

### S-Curve Analysis

**One-paragraph description:** Technology and product performance over time tends to follow an S-curve: slow start, rapid improvement, plateau. Multiple competing technologies each have their own S-curve.

**When to use:** When deciding whether to invest in extending current technology vs jumping to a new one.

**The pattern:** When current technology is on its plateau (no more easy gains), it's time to consider jumping to the next S-curve — even though the new tech may *currently* be performing worse than the mature current tech.

**Example:** Sail → steam → diesel → gas turbine. Each transition involved jumping to a lower-performing technology that had more upside.

---

### Stage-Gate Process

**Originator:** Robert G. Cooper (1980s)

**One-paragraph description:** A structured product development process with sequential stages (idea, scoping, business case, development, testing, launch) separated by "gates" where projects are reviewed and either continued, killed, returned, or held.

**When to use:** For new product development pipelines; for innovation portfolio management.

**Strength:** Disciplined kill criteria prevent endless investment in low-potential projects.

**Critique:** Can slow down agile / iterative work; rigid gates inappropriate for some innovation types.

---

### Lean Startup

**Originator:** Eric Ries (2011)

**One-paragraph description:** A method for developing new products/businesses through Build-Measure-Learn cycles with Minimum Viable Products (MVPs) to validate assumptions. Pivot if learning contradicts hypothesis; persevere if it confirms.

**When to use:** For new ventures, internal innovation, products with high market uncertainty.

**Key concepts:**
- MVP (Minimum Viable Product) — smallest thing you can build to test core assumption
- Validated Learning — actual data, not opinion, validates assumptions
- Pivot / Persevere — change direction based on what learning shows
- Innovation Accounting — metrics for early-stage ventures (where revenue and profit aren't yet meaningful)

---

## 8. Organizational design

### Galbraith Star Model

**Originator:** Jay Galbraith (1977)

**One-paragraph description:** Five organizational design levers that must align: Strategy, Structure, Processes, Rewards, People. All five must be designed together (changing one without the others creates dysfunction).

**When to use:** When redesigning an organization; when explaining why a strategy isn't being executed; in post-merger integration.

**The five points of the star:**
- **Strategy** — Direction and goals
- **Structure** — Power, hierarchy, departmentalization
- **Processes** — Decision rights, information flows
- **Rewards** — Compensation, recognition, incentives
- **People** — Skills, hiring, development

**Relationship to 7-S:** Galbraith Star is similar to 7-S but more execution-focused. 7-S emphasizes culture and shared values more.

---

### Burke-Litwin Causal Model

**Originator:** W. Warner Burke & George Litwin (1992)

**One-paragraph description:** A complex causal model of organizational performance with 12 elements arranged in transformational vs transactional factors. External environment drives transformational factors (mission, leadership, culture), which drive transactional factors (management, work climate, motivation), which drive performance.

**When to use:** When diagnosing why an organization is underperforming; when planning major organizational change.

**Strength:** Distinguishes transformational from transactional change (different leaders, methods, timing).

**Why it's less popular than 7-S:** More complex (12 factors), less visual.

---

### Cynefin Framework

**Originator:** Dave Snowden (1999)

**One-paragraph description:** A sense-making framework distinguishing five domains for decision-making contexts: Simple (Clear), Complicated, Complex, Chaotic, and Disorder. Different approaches work for each.

**When to use:** When leaders aren't sure how to approach a problem — especially novel, ambiguous situations.

**The domains:**

| Domain | Cause-effect | Approach | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple / Clear** | Obvious, known | Sense → Categorize → Respond. Best practices. | Order processing |
| **Complicated** | Knowable through expertise | Sense → Analyze → Respond. Good practices. | Building a bridge |
| **Complex** | Only knowable in retrospect | Probe → Sense → Respond. Emergent practice. | Raising a child, building a startup |
| **Chaotic** | No cause-effect | Act → Sense → Respond. Novel practice. | Crisis response |
| **Disorder** | Unclear which domain | Decompose into other domains | — |

**Key insight:** Most management training assumes Complicated domain — expert analysis solves it. Complex domains require experimentation. Chaotic domains require action first, analysis later.

**Common mistakes:** Treating Complex problems as Complicated (over-analyzing what can only be learned by doing); treating Complicated as Simple (deferring to "best practice" instead of expert analysis).

---

### Spotify Model (Squads, Tribes, Chapters, Guilds)

**Originator:** Spotify engineering (2012, Henrik Kniberg)

**One-paragraph description:** An agile organizational model: small cross-functional Squads (8 people), grouped into Tribes (~100), with Chapters (functional groupings across squads) and Guilds (interest-based communities).

**When to use:** For tech / product organizations trying to combine agile autonomy with alignment.

**Critique:** Spotify itself abandoned the strict model; widely copied superficially without the underlying culture. Use as inspiration, not a prescription.

---

### Tuckman's Stages of Group Development

**Originator:** Bruce Tuckman (1965)

**One-paragraph description:** Five stages teams progress through: Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing → Adjourning.

**When to use:** When building new teams; when diagnosing why a team isn't performing.

**Insight:** "Storming" is necessary, not avoidable — teams that skip conflict end up with surface harmony and poor decision quality.

---

## 9. Change management

### Kotter's 8-Step Change Model

**Originator:** John Kotter (1995)

**One-paragraph description:** Eight sequential steps for leading change: Create urgency → Form coalition → Develop vision → Communicate vision → Empower action → Generate short-term wins → Sustain acceleration → Anchor change.

**When to use:** For major organizational change initiatives (restructuring, M&A integration, transformation).

**The 8 steps:**
1. Create a sense of urgency
2. Build a guiding coalition
3. Form a strategic vision and initiatives
4. Enlist a volunteer army
5. Enable action by removing barriers
6. Generate short-term wins
7. Sustain acceleration
8. Institute change

**Common failure modes (Kotter's research):** Skipping urgency (people don't act); declaring victory too soon (step 8 not reached, change rolls back).

---

### Lewin's 3-Stage Model (Unfreeze-Change-Refreeze)

**Originator:** Kurt Lewin (1947)

**One-paragraph description:** Change happens in three stages: Unfreeze (create dissatisfaction with current state, motivate change), Change (move to new state), Refreeze (lock in the change with new norms, structures, rewards).

**When to use:** As a high-level frame for any change initiative. Underlies most other change models.

**Strength:** Simplicity. The Refreeze stage reminds change leaders that change isn't done until it's institutionalized.

---

### ADKAR Model

**Originator:** Jeff Hiatt / Prosci (2003)

**One-paragraph description:** Individual-level change model: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement. Each person needs all five to successfully change.

**When to use:** When change requires individual behavior change (not just structural change).

**The five stages (per individual):**
- **Awareness** of the need for change
- **Desire** to participate and support the change
- **Knowledge** of how to change
- **Ability** to implement the required skills
- **Reinforcement** to sustain the change

**Insight:** A change initiative may fail because individuals are stuck at one ADKAR stage (e.g., they have Awareness but no Desire). The intervention needs to match the stage.

---

### Bridges Transition Model

**Originator:** William Bridges (1991)

**One-paragraph description:** Distinguishes *change* (the external event) from *transition* (the internal psychological process). Transition has three phases: Ending (letting go of the old), Neutral Zone (chaotic in-between), New Beginning (embracing the new).

**When to use:** When change leaders are frustrated that change isn't sticking despite all the structural elements being in place.

**Insight:** Most change leaders focus on the external change. The psychological transition lags. Resistance is often grief, not opposition.

---

## 10. Decision-making

### RACI Matrix (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed)

**One-paragraph description:** Clarifies roles in decision-making or task execution: who is Responsible (does the work), Accountable (signs off), Consulted (provides input before), Informed (told after).

**When to use:** When there's confusion about who decides what; for project management; for cross-functional initiatives.

**Rules:**
- Exactly one Accountable per task (otherwise no one is)
- Multiple Responsible OK
- Multiple Consulted OK
- Multiple Informed OK

---

### RAPID (Bain)

**Originator:** Bain & Company

**One-paragraph description:** Bain's decision rights framework: Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide. Used for clarifying complex cross-functional decisions.

**The roles:**
- **Recommend** — Proposes the decision
- **Agree** — Has veto power
- **Perform** — Executes the decision
- **Input** — Provides input (no veto)
- **Decide** — Makes the final call

**Use when:** RACI feels too coarse; multiple stakeholders need different levels of decision involvement.

---

### DACI Framework

**One-paragraph description:** Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed. Similar to RACI but the Driver is project-facilitating (not the doer).

---

### Eisenhower Matrix (Urgent / Important)

**Originator:** Attributed to Dwight Eisenhower; popularized by Stephen Covey (1989)

**One-paragraph description:** 2×2 prioritization matrix: Urgent (Y) × Important (X). Four quadrants: Do First (urgent + important), Schedule (important not urgent), Delegate (urgent not important), Eliminate (neither).

**When to use:** Personal productivity, project prioritization.

**Insight:** Most people spend too much time on urgent but not important. The leverage is in the important but not urgent quadrant (planning, prevention, strategy).

---

### Decision Tree

**One-paragraph description:** A branching diagram showing decisions, possible outcomes, probabilities, and payoffs. Used to compute expected value of decisions.

**When to use:** When the decision involves uncertainty with quantifiable probabilities.

**Steps:**
1. Draw decision nodes (squares) and chance nodes (circles)
2. Assign probabilities to each chance outcome
3. Assign payoffs to each terminal branch
4. Compute expected value by rolling back from right to left

---

## 11. Problem-solving

### 5 Whys

**Originator:** Sakichi Toyoda (Toyota, 1930s)

**One-paragraph description:** Ask "why?" five times in sequence to drill from a symptom to the root cause.

**When to use:** When investigating a problem's root cause. Especially for operational / quality issues.

**Example:** "Machine stopped" → why? → "Fuse blew" → why? → "Bearing failed from lack of lubricant" → why? → "Pump not pumping enough oil" → why? → "Pump shaft worn" → why? → "No strainer; metal scrap getting into pump." Root cause: missing strainer, not the fuse.

**Common mistakes:** Stopping too early (at a symptom, not a cause); accepting "human error" as a root cause (almost always there's a deeper systemic cause).

---

### Fishbone / Ishikawa Diagram

**Originator:** Kaoru Ishikawa (1968)

**One-paragraph description:** A cause-and-effect diagram shaped like a fish skeleton: the problem is the head; major cause categories are the bones; specific causes branch from each bone.

**When to use:** For complex problems with multiple potential causes; in quality management; for brainstorming root causes systematically.

**Standard cause categories (6M for manufacturing):**
- Methods
- Machines
- Materials
- Manpower (people)
- Measurement
- Mother Nature (environment)

**For services (3P):** People, Process, Policy. Or: People, Process, Tools, Environment.

**Strength:** Surfaces all categories of cause; prevents fixation on one type.

---

### SIPOC (Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs, Customers)

**One-paragraph description:** A high-level process map showing Suppliers (who provides inputs), Inputs (what's used), Process (the steps), Outputs (what's produced), Customers (who consumes the outputs).

**When to use:** At the start of process improvement initiatives, before doing detailed value stream mapping. Six Sigma DMAIC tool.

---

### Kepner-Tregoe Problem Analysis

**Originator:** Charles Kepner & Benjamin Tregoe (1958)

**One-paragraph description:** A structured rational process for problem analysis: define the problem precisely (is/is not), find distinctions, find changes, identify possible causes, test most probable cause.

**When to use:** For complex problems where the cause isn't obvious; in technical / operational diagnostics.

**Four components:**
- Situation Appraisal — what's actually happening
- Problem Analysis — finding root cause
- Decision Analysis — choosing course of action
- Potential Problem Analysis — what could go wrong with the chosen course

---

### Pre-mortem

**Originator:** Gary Klein

**One-paragraph description:** Before launching an initiative, imagine it has failed catastrophically. Then ask: "Why did it fail?" Brainstorm causes. Use them to strengthen the plan before execution.

**When to use:** Before any major decision or initiative.

**Why it works:** Hindsight imagination produces more candid risk identification than prospective brainstorming. Once people imagine failure, they're freed to identify risks they previously suppressed.

---

### Six Thinking Hats (De Bono)

**Originator:** Edward de Bono (1985)

**One-paragraph description:** A parallel-thinking method where a group adopts one of six "hats" representing different thinking modes, in sequence: White (facts), Red (feelings), Black (caution), Yellow (optimism), Green (creativity), Blue (process).

**When to use:** For group brainstorming, especially with mixed-discipline teams or in situations where one mode of thinking dominates.

**The six hats:**
- **White** — Information, data, facts
- **Red** — Feelings, intuition, hunches
- **Black** — Judgment, caution, risks
- **Yellow** — Optimism, benefits, value
- **Green** — Creativity, alternatives, new ideas
- **Blue** — Process, control of thinking

---

### A3 Thinking (Toyota)

**Originator:** Toyota (1960s)

**One-paragraph description:** Problem-solving methodology requiring everything to fit on a single A3-sized paper (11"×17"): Background → Current Condition → Goal → Analysis → Countermeasures → Plan → Follow-up.

**When to use:** For continuous improvement (kaizen) work; when wanting forced concision in problem documentation.

**Strength:** Forces discipline. If it doesn't fit on A3, you haven't thought it through.

---

## 12. Risk & scenario

### Scenario Planning

**Originator:** Royal Dutch Shell (Pierre Wack, 1970s)

**One-paragraph description:** Develop 2–4 distinct, plausible, and consequential future scenarios. Test strategy against each. Build resilience by finding strategies that work across scenarios.

**When to use:** For long-horizon strategic planning under deep uncertainty (geopolitics, energy, climate, technology).

**Standard process:**
1. Identify critical uncertainties (high impact, high uncertainty)
2. Pick the top 2 critical uncertainties
3. Build a 2×2 matrix of high/low on each
4. Develop a narrative for each quadrant
5. Stress-test current strategy against each scenario
6. Identify strategies that perform well across scenarios

**Example:** Shell's 1970s scenarios identified an "energy crisis" possibility years before OPEC's oil embargo. Shell was prepared.

**Common mistakes:** Building scenarios around things you can already predict (use scenarios for things you can't); too many scenarios (4 is the maximum manageable); not following through to strategy implications.

---

### Risk Heat Map

**One-paragraph description:** 2D matrix: Likelihood × Impact. Risks plotted as dots/circles. Top-right quadrant = highest priority for mitigation.

**When to use:** For project risk management; for ERM (Enterprise Risk Management).

**Variations:**
- Add color (green/yellow/red) for quick read
- Add second matrix showing residual risk after mitigation
- Use 5×5 grid for finer gradation

---

### Black Swan Thinking (Taleb)

**Originator:** Nassim Nicholas Taleb (2007)

**One-paragraph description:** Recognition that the most consequential events are often unpredictable outliers (Black Swans) that look obvious only in retrospect. Strategy should focus on resilience to unknown risks rather than precise prediction of known risks.

**When to use:** When the firm is over-confident about predictability; when designing risk management for fat-tailed environments (finance, technology, geopolitics).

**Key concepts:**
- **Black Swan** — Rare, high-impact, retrospectively predictable
- **Antifragility** — Beyond resilience: systems that gain from disorder
- **Skin in the game** — Decision-makers should bear consequences

---

### Real Options Reasoning

**One-paragraph description:** Treats strategic investments as options — small initial investments preserve the option to scale up if conditions become favorable, or abandon if unfavorable. Adapted from financial options.

**When to use:** When evaluating staged R&D investments, market entry experiments, JV / partnership decisions.

**Key insight:** Net Present Value (NPV) understates the value of investments under uncertainty because it ignores managerial flexibility. Real options value that flexibility.

**Types of real options:**
- **Option to defer** — wait before committing
- **Option to expand** — small bet, scale if good
- **Option to abandon** — exit if bad
- **Option to switch** — change between modes (e.g., production vs distribution)

**Common mistakes:** Applying complex Black-Scholes math when a simple decision tree is enough; treating every investment as having option value (most don't have managerial flexibility).

---

## 13. Operations & process

### Lean / Toyota Production System

**Originator:** Taiichi Ohno, Eiji Toyoda (Toyota, 1950s onward)

**One-paragraph description:** A management philosophy focused on eliminating waste (muda) and producing value efficiently. Key principles: pull production, just-in-time inventory, kaizen (continuous improvement), respect for people.

**When to use:** For manufacturing or services with repeatable processes and waste reduction opportunities.

**Seven wastes (8 with non-utilized talent):**
1. Transportation
2. Inventory
3. Motion
4. Waiting
5. Over-processing
6. Overproduction
7. Defects
8. Non-utilized talent (added later)

Mnemonic: TIMWOOD.

---

### Six Sigma DMAIC

**Originator:** Motorola (1980s), popularized by GE (Jack Welch)

**One-paragraph description:** A data-driven quality improvement methodology with five phases: Define → Measure → Analyze → Improve → Control. Targets <3.4 defects per million opportunities ("six sigma").

**When to use:** For quality improvement in repeatable processes with measurable outcomes.

**The five phases:**
- **Define** — Project scope, customer needs, goals
- **Measure** — Current performance, data collection plan
- **Analyze** — Root cause analysis with statistical tools
- **Improve** — Develop and test solutions
- **Control** — Lock in improvements, monitor, prevent regression

**Key tools:** SIPOC, Fishbone, FMEA, Control charts, Hypothesis testing, Design of Experiments.

---

### Theory of Constraints (Goldratt)

**Originator:** Eli Goldratt (1984, *The Goal*)

**One-paragraph description:** Every system has one constraint (bottleneck) limiting throughput. Continuous improvement requires identifying the constraint, exploiting it (squeezing maximum out), subordinating everything else to it, and elevating it (adding capacity). Then the next constraint emerges.

**When to use:** For operations / supply chain / process problems where throughput matters.

**The five focusing steps:**
1. Identify the constraint
2. Exploit the constraint (maximize its output)
3. Subordinate everything else to the constraint
4. Elevate the constraint (invest to increase its capacity)
5. Go back to step 1 (the constraint will have moved)

**Strength:** Counters the instinct to improve every step (often wastes effort if not at the constraint).

---

### Value Stream Mapping (VSM)

**One-paragraph description:** A lean visualization tool showing every step in a process — both value-adding and non-value-adding — with cycle times, lead times, inventory, and information flows.

**When to use:** As the diagnostic step in any lean transformation.

**Standard symbols:** Process boxes, inventory triangles, push arrows, FIFO lanes, data boxes with cycle time / changeover / uptime.

**Output:** Current state map + Future state map + Improvement plan.

---

### Little's Law

**One-paragraph description:** L = λW. The average number of items in a queueing system (L) equals the average arrival rate (λ) multiplied by the average time an item spends in the system (W).

**When to use:** For analyzing inventory, queues, work-in-process, lead times.

**Application:** If lead time is 30 days and you want inventory of 100 units, throughput must be 100/30 ≈ 3.3 units/day. Or — to reduce inventory at constant throughput, reduce lead time.

---

### OEE (Overall Equipment Effectiveness)

**One-paragraph description:** OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality. The composite metric for manufacturing effectiveness. World-class is typically 85%+.

**When to use:** For manufacturing operations improvement.

**Components:**
- **Availability** — Run time / Planned production time
- **Performance** — Actual output / Theoretical max output at run time
- **Quality** — Good units / Total units produced

**Strength:** Single number captures multiple operational dimensions.

---

### 5S

**Originator:** Toyota (Japan)

**One-paragraph description:** Workplace organization method: Sort (Seiri), Set in Order (Seiton), Shine (Seiso), Standardize (Seiketsu), Sustain (Shitsuke).

**When to use:** As foundational lean discipline in any operation.

**Often the first lean intervention** — gets the workplace organized before more complex improvements.

---

## 14. International & global

### CAGE Framework (Ghemawat)

**Originator:** Pankaj Ghemawat (2001)

**One-paragraph description:** Four dimensions of distance between countries that affect international business: Cultural, Administrative, Geographic, Economic.

**When to use:** When entering a foreign market; when assessing why an international strategy is failing.

**The four CAGE dimensions:**

| Distance | Drivers |
|---|---|
| **Cultural** | Language, religion, ethnicity, social norms, values |
| **Administrative** | Political, legal, regulatory institutions; lack of treaties; corruption |
| **Geographic** | Physical distance, time zones, climate differences, infrastructure |
| **Economic** | Income levels, costs of inputs, infrastructure quality |

**Strength:** Goes beyond geographic distance to capture the real difficulties of cross-border operations.

**Example:** US entering Mexico — geographically close, but culturally / administratively distant. Australia entering New Zealand — distant geographically, but close on all other dimensions.

---

### AAA Framework (Ghemawat)

**Originator:** Pankaj Ghemawat (2007)

**One-paragraph description:** Three global strategies: Adaptation (modify products for local needs), Aggregation (achieve scale by grouping similar markets), Arbitrage (exploit differences between markets).

**When to use:** When designing a global strategy. Companies typically lead on one A; combining all three is rare and risky.

**The three A's:**

| Strategy | Approach | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Adaptation** | Customize to local conditions | Unilever (local product variants), McDonald's (local menu items) |
| **Aggregation** | Find similarities across markets, achieve scale | Boeing (global product), Toyota (regional manufacturing) |
| **Arbitrage** | Exploit national differences | Wipro (low-cost India + high-value services to US), Walmart (global sourcing) |

**Common mistakes:** Trying to do all three (creates organizational complexity beyond capabilities); not picking one as the leading strategy.

---

### Transnational Strategy Matrix (Bartlett & Ghoshal)

**Originator:** Christopher Bartlett & Sumantra Ghoshal (1989)

**One-paragraph description:** 2×2 matrix: Pressure for Global Integration (X) × Pressure for Local Responsiveness (Y). Four strategies: International (low/low), Multidomestic (low integration / high local), Global (high integration / low local), Transnational (both high).

**When to use:** When choosing the right international strategy archetype.

**The four:**

| | Local responsiveness Low | Local responsiveness High |
|---|---|---|
| Global integration Low | **International** (export model) | **Multidomestic** (decentralized, local subsidiaries) |
| Global integration High | **Global** (standardized worldwide) | **Transnational** (integrated + locally adapted) |

**Insight:** Transnational is hardest to execute but often required in mature global industries. Most companies are stuck oscillating.

## 15. Performance management

### Balanced Scorecard

**Originator:** Robert Kaplan & David Norton (1992, HBR)

**One-paragraph description:** A performance measurement framework with four perspectives: Financial, Customer, Internal Processes, Learning & Growth. Each has objectives, measures, targets, initiatives.

**When to use:** For strategy execution; when financial metrics alone are inadequate.

**The four perspectives:**
- **Financial** — How do we look to shareholders?
- **Customer** — How do customers see us?
- **Internal Processes** — What must we excel at?
- **Learning & Growth** — How do we continue to improve?

**Strategy map:** Causal chain from Learning & Growth → Process → Customer → Financial. Forces explicit theory of how value is created.

**Common mistakes:** Too many metrics (defeats the purpose); using BSC as a measurement system only (without strategy maps); ignoring the causal links.

---

### OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)

**Originator:** Andy Grove (Intel, 1970s); popularized by John Doerr / Google

**One-paragraph description:** A goal-setting system: Objectives (qualitative, ambitious) with 3–5 Key Results (quantitative, measurable) per objective. Cascade across organizational levels.

**When to use:** For high-growth tech / product orgs; when alignment + ambition + measurement need to happen together.

**Rules:**
- Objectives are inspirational, action-oriented qualitative
- Key Results are specific, measurable, ambitious quantitative
- 3–5 KRs per Objective
- 60–70% achievement is considered success (otherwise too easy)
- OKRs are public across the organization

**Example:**
- Objective: Become the most beloved coffee shop in the neighborhood
- KR1: Increase NPS from 45 to 70
- KR2: Increase Yelp rating from 4.2 to 4.8
- KR3: Increase repeat customer rate from 30% to 60%

---

### Hoshin Kanri (Strategy Deployment)

**Originator:** Japan (Toyota, Komatsu, others, 1960s)

**One-paragraph description:** A strategic planning method that cascades long-term breakthrough objectives through the organization with bi-directional dialogue (catchball) to ensure alignment and resource commitment.

**When to use:** For companies wanting tighter integration between strategic plan and operational execution. Common in lean organizations.

**Key elements:**
- **Hoshin** — strategic breakthrough objective (3–5 year)
- **Catchball** — back-and-forth refinement between levels
- **X-Matrix** — visualizes strategy at each level
- **Bowler chart** — monthly tracking against targets

**Difference from OKRs:** Hoshin is slower, more deliberate, used for breakthrough strategy. OKRs are quarterly, agile, used for execution.

---

### KPI Trees

**One-paragraph description:** A hierarchical decomposition of a top-level KPI (e.g., profit) into its mathematical drivers, recursively, until you reach controllable operational levers.

**When to use:** When designing dashboards; when identifying which metrics matter at which level.

**Example:** Profit → Revenue − Cost → (Volume × Price) − (Variable cost + Fixed cost) → Volume = Customers × Frequency × Order size → Customers = New + Existing − Churned → etc.

Each leaf is a metric a specific team can move.

---

## 16. Niche & advanced

### Strategic Group Mapping

**One-paragraph description:** A 2D map plotting competitors against two strategic dimensions (e.g., price level × product range). Clusters of competitors form "strategic groups." Competition is more intense within groups than across.

**When to use:** When mapping competitive structure in an industry with diverse competitors. Reveals white space and mobility barriers.

**How it works:**
1. Identify two key strategic dimensions
2. Plot all competitors
3. Cluster into strategic groups
4. Identify mobility barriers between groups
5. Find white space (positions no group occupies)

**Common mistakes:** Picking dimensions that are correlated (e.g., price × quality); using only two dimensions when three or more matter.

---

### Stakeholder Power-Interest Grid

**Originator:** Aubrey Mendelow (1991)

**One-paragraph description:** 2×2 grid mapping stakeholders by Power (Y) × Interest (X). Four quadrants suggest engagement strategies: Manage Closely (high/high), Keep Satisfied (high power / low interest), Keep Informed (low power / high interest), Monitor (low/low).

**When to use:** For change management, M&A integration, controversial decisions, public-affairs strategy.

---

### McKinsey Influence Model

**Originator:** McKinsey (Lawson & Price, 2003)

**One-paragraph description:** Four behavioral interventions that drive change in mindsets and behaviors: Fostering understanding & conviction (role-modeling), Reinforcing with formal mechanisms, Developing talent & skills, Role-modeling.

**When to use:** For behavior-change programs; for culture transformation.

**The four influences:**
1. **Compelling story** — People understand what's being asked and why
2. **Role modeling** — They see leaders changing their own behavior
3. **Formal mechanisms** — Structure, process, incentives align
4. **Capabilities** — They have the skills to make the change

**Insight:** All four must be present. Missing any one undermines the change.

---

### Profit Pool Analysis (Bain)

**Originator:** Bain & Company (Gadiesh & Gilbert, 1998)

**One-paragraph description:** A way of analyzing where profit is concentrated across an industry's value chain. Reveals that profit pools are often very different from revenue pools — value capture concentrates at specific stages.

**When to use:** When deciding where to play in an industry's value chain; when explaining why some industries have wildly different profitability across stages.

**How it works:**
1. Define the value chain (stages from raw input to consumer)
2. Estimate revenue at each stage
3. Estimate profit margin at each stage
4. Visualize as a stacked area chart: revenue at each stage × margin
5. Identify the profit pools — where the profit concentrates

**Example:** US auto value chain — manufacturing gets most revenue but financing / leasing / repair get most profit. Financing was a profit pool ignored for years before captive lenders emerged.

---

### DuPont Analysis (ROE Decomposition)

**Originator:** DuPont Corporation (1920s)

**One-paragraph description:** Decomposes Return on Equity (ROE) into three components: Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier. Reveals what's driving (or hurting) ROE.

**When to use:** For financial analysis; for understanding what's driving a competitor's superior or inferior returns.

**The formula:**
ROE = (Net Income / Revenue) × (Revenue / Assets) × (Assets / Equity)
    = Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier

**Strength:** Allows comparison across companies with very different business models — high-margin / low-turnover vs low-margin / high-turnover can have the same ROE.

**Extended DuPont:** 5-step decomposition adding tax burden and interest burden.

---

### McKinsey 4×4 Plant the Seeds Matrix (a-typical use)

Generic name: many adjacent variations exist for prioritization matrices. Mention briefly: cost-benefit grid, prioritization 2×2, ICE (Impact × Confidence × Ease).

## How to layer frameworks

Most real strategic analyses combine multiple frameworks. Some common layering patterns:

| Combo | Use case |
|---|---|
| PESTLE + Porter's 5 Forces + Strategic Group Mapping | Comprehensive industry / competitive analysis |
| 7-S + Galbraith Star | Organizational design with culture lens |
| VRIO + 7 Powers | Sustainable advantage assessment |
| BCG + GE 9-Box + Three Horizons | Portfolio management |
| Ansoff + Blue Ocean + CAGE | Growth strategy with international and innovation lenses |
| Customer Journey + Service Blueprint + JTBD | Service / experience design |
| Kotter + ADKAR + Influence Model | Change management at organization and individual levels |
| SIPOC + Value Stream Mapping + Theory of Constraints | Operations diagnosis |
| Issue Tree + MECE + Pyramid Principle | Problem structuring and communication |

**Heuristic:** Use 2–4 frameworks for an analysis. Fewer = potentially missing dimensions. More = framework salad, no synthesis.
