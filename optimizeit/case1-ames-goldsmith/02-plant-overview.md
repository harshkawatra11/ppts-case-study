160 years. **Ames Chemical Company** was founded in **1860** by Merritt Ames (1825–1914), a
Vermont-born chemist and photographer, whose original business was collecting and purifying
silver-bearing residue discarded by commercial photographers — silver having been the active
light-sensitive agent in photographic emulsion and film processing [VERIFIED] [S-01]. The operation
was sold to **GAF Corporation** in the late 1960s and was re-formed as **Ames Goldsmith Corporation
in 1979** [VERIFIED] [S-01]. Ames Goldsmith's global headquarters sits at 50 Harrison Avenue, South
Glens Falls, New York [VERIFIED] [S-02].

The company describes itself, on its own corporate site, as "one of the largest integrated silver
fabricators in the world," with annual silver throughput "well in excess of 85 million troy ounces,"
and cites nearly 150 years of continuous silver-recycling operation [VERIFIED, corporate self-report]
[S-01]. Its stated business lines extend well beyond the WV plant's refining function: silver
nitrate production (>10 tons/day, per corporate claim), silver oxide production (>15 tons/day),
silver powders and flakes, copper-based materials, and PEM electrolyser/fuel-cell catalyst materials
under the "HyPer" brand [VERIFIED, corporate self-report] [S-03]. The corporate timeline includes a
2013 metal-powders expansion, the 2015 acquisition of Ceimig Limited (UK), and 2017 integration of
Colonial Metals Industries [VERIFIED] [S-01]. Notably, Ames Goldsmith's own public "About Us" page
does **not** mention West Virginia, Institute, Nitro, or "Catalyst Refiners" anywhere — the corporate
parent's public history is silent on the WV subsidiary specifically, and every WV-specific
operational fact in this chapter had to be sourced from third-party directory listings, trade-press
coverage, and post-incident news, not from the company's own institutional history [VERIFIED] [S-01].
Ames Goldsmith also appears in a corporate-finance database as the subject of an equity

retrieved in this research pass and are flagged as an open item, not asserted here [INFERRED — lead

Within this corporate structure, the relevant refining service line is **Ethylene Oxide (EO) catalyst

## 2.3 Why Silver Is Used as an Ethylene Oxide Catalyst

Silver's role in this facility's business is not incidental — it is the reason spent-catalyst
refining exists as a business line at all, and it is directly responsible for the presence of
sulfide-bearing residue that later chapters identify as a proximate hazard.

Silver is the industrially dominant catalytic metal for **direct vapor-phase epoxidation of ethylene
to ethylene oxide**:

```
C2H4 + 1/2 O2  --[Ag catalyst]-->  C2H4O   (ethylene oxide)
```

Silver is essentially the only metal that gives commercially useful **selectivity** toward the
epoxide product rather than full combustion of ethylene to CO2 and H2O. Modern EO-grade catalysts —
silver deposited on a low-surface-area alpha-alumina (Al2O3) support, typically promoted with
alkali metals and rhenium — can reach selectivity approaching roughly 90% [VERIFIED, general
catalysis literature] [S-10].

### Sulfur poisoning: how catalyst becomes "spent"

Sulfur-containing species — H2S, carbonyl sulfide (COS), mercaptans, and related compounds — are
well-documented **poisons** for silver EO catalysts. Even trace levels in the ethylene/oxygen feed
chemisorb onto the silver surface and convert metallic silver to **silver sulfide (Ag2S)**:

```
2 Ag + H2S  -->  Ag2S + H2         (or analogous oxidation-reduction with COS, mercaptans, etc.)
```

Ag2S is catalytically inactive and effectively irreversible under normal process conditions — its
solubility product is extremely low (Ksp ~= 6.3 x 10^-50), so once sulfur contacts the silver
surface the reaction proceeds essentially to completion there [VERIFIED, general catalysis/
surface-chemistry literature] [S-11]. This is precisely why EO plants install a **sulfur guard bed**
upstream of the reactor, at ambient temperature, specifically to strip sulfur species out of the
ethylene feed before it ever reaches the silver catalyst [VERIFIED, general process-design
literature] [S-10].

**This chemistry is the direct, load-bearing link to the facility's residue inventory.** Because
sulfur poisoning is one of the principal end-of-life deactivation mechanisms for EO silver catalyst
(alongside normal sintering/surface-area loss and chloride-moderator effects), spent EO silver
catalyst arriving at a reclamation facility as routine feedstock can be expected to carry some
fraction of its silver content as Ag2S rather than as pure metallic silver [INFERRED — general
catalyst-chemistry reasoning, not a plant-specific finding, per lane3 research] [S-11]. The direct
consequence for a plant that processes this feedstock as its core business: **a refiner receiving
spent EO catalyst as routine input should be expected, as a matter of ordinary operation, to
accumulate sulfide-bearing residues and sludges somewhere in its process inventory and waste
streams** — independently of any accident scenario. This is the mechanism by which "silver mud"
sitting in a legacy vessel at a plant scheduled for decommissioning is not an anomaly requiring
special explanation; it is the expected residue of the plant's normal decades-long function.

## 2.4 Generic Industrial Process: Spent-Catalyst Silver Recovery

Patent and technical literature on recovering silver (and associated promoter metals such as
rhenium) from spent Ag/alpha-Al2O3 EO catalyst converges on a consistent set of process elements
[VERIFIED, patent/technical literature synthesis — S-12 through S-17]:

1. **Physical preparation** — grinding/comminution of spent catalyst pellets to expose surface area.
2. **Optional oxidative pretreatment** — some routes roast the ground catalyst in a mixed gas
   atmosphere (chlorine/alkane/oxygen/nitrogen) to convert residual chlorides and organics and
   improve subsequent leaching.
3. **Acid leaching — the core recovery step.** The near-universal industrial approach is to strip
   and dissolve silver from the ground catalyst using an acid in which silver is soluble — classically
   **nitric acid (HNO3)**, sometimes with hydrogen peroxide added as an oxidant to help dissolve
   less-reactive silver species (explicitly including Ag2S from sulfur-poisoned catalyst, per §2.3
   above). This yields (a) a silver-bearing "pregnant" leach solution/filtrate and (b) a spent-alumina
   filter-cake residue.
4. **Solution purification** — filtration and impurity removal from the pregnant leach solution, in
   some routes with an intermediate precipitation step (e.g., silver chloride via HCl addition).
5. **Electrolytic refining (Moebius-type process)** — the industry-standard method for high-purity
   silver recovery. Impure silver anodes are suspended in an electrolyte bath (typically silver
   nitrate + nitric acid solution); thin silver or stainless-steel cathodes accept deposited pure
   silver under applied DC current (anode: Ag -> Ag+ + e-; cathode: Ag+ + e- -> Ag). Base-metal
   impurities either remain in solution or settle to the tank bottom as **anode slime**, commonly
   called **"silver mud."**
6. **Electrolyte management** — copper is a common contaminant that accumulates in the silver
   nitrate/nitric acid electrolyte over repeated refining cycles; industry practice caps electrolyte
   copper at roughly 60 g/L, above which copper co-deposits at the cathode and degrades product
   purity. This forces refiners to periodically bleed, regenerate, or replace nitric-acid-based
   electrolyte — itself generating a nitric-acid-bearing waste/recovery stream [VERIFIED, patent
   literature] [S-16].

Reported total silver-recovery rates in the patent/technical literature commonly range from 98% to
above 99.7% [VERIFIED, patent literature] [S-12]. Ames Goldsmith's own facility listing confirms it
uses "electrolytic refining technology" for silver reclamation at the WV site, which ties this
generic Moebius-type description directly to the plant's stated capability — though the listing does
not name the Moebius process specifically or disclose a proprietary flowsheet [VERIFIED, corporate
self-report, partial correspondence only] [S-05].

## 2.7 Bonderite M2000A: What Is and Is Not Known

This section is deliberately narrow because the underlying data could not be obtained.

**What is confirmed:** Bonderite is a real, current Henkel industrial product line. A specific
product, **"BONDERITE(R) M-AD 2000A,"** exists with a January 2020 technical data sheet hosted on
Henkel's own datasheet portal, described in file metadata as a "liquid additive" for metal
pre-treatment [VERIFIED — TDS located and its existence confirmed] [S-21]. Critically, **it is not
confirmed that "M2000A" as named in incident coverage is the same product as "BONDERITE M-AD 2000A."**
Three possibilities remain open: (a) press shorthand/misreporting of the M-AD 2000A name, (b) a
distinct, similarly-named product not indexed by the search tools used in this research, or (c) an
internal/site-specific abbreviation for a Henkel product ordered under a different catalog
designation. None of these could be ruled in or out.

**What could not be obtained:** the technical data sheet PDF for BONDERITE M-AD 2000A was retrieved
but its content is stored in compressed internal PDF text streams that a standard text-extraction pass
could not decode; the full ingredient/composition table was **not successfully extracted** in this
research pass [S-21]. No safety data sheet (SDS) — the document that would actually list hazardous
ingredients — was located for either name at all.

**What is known about the general product family** (not confirmed as this specific SKU's
composition): Henkel's Bonderite cleaner/pretreatment line is marketed for removing machining and
grinding soils while providing simultaneous corrosion protection, for use in spray-washer, immersion,
vibratory, and ultrasonic cleaning systems, primarily on ferrous and aluminum alloys [VERIFIED,
distributor product-family description, not SKU-specific] [S-22]. Products in this family are
generally described as alkaline-based cleaners formulated with alkalinity agents, corrosion
inhibitors, and detergents/surfactants; the "M-AD" prefix in Henkel's catalog generally denotes
"pretreatment additive" products as distinct from "M-NT" neutralizers or "M-PT" phosphate/
conversion-coating products [VERIFIED, general Henkel product-line naming convention, not confirmed
against an official Henkel legend] [S-22]. A pulmonary physician quoted in post-incident coverage
characterized the substance functionally, not by ingredient list, as "a pulmonary irritant that coats
lung tissue and impedes oxygen absorption" [VERIFIED, quoted attribution] [S-23]. Coverage also states
that mixing nitric acid with M2000A during tank decommissioning was "not an unusual part of
decontamination procedures" at the site historically [VERIFIED, single-source characterization]
[S-23].

One outlet's own framing — that "the combination of nitric acid with certain organic or
sulfur-containing compounds can generate hydrogen sulfide as a byproduct" — implies the reporter's
own assumption that M2000A likely contained sulfur-bearing material. **This is the reporter's
inference, not a confirmed ingredient disclosure, and must be treated as unverified** [HYPOTHESIS,
attributed to a news outlet's own reasoning, not evidence]. Chapter 4's reaction-chemistry analysis
therefore carries forward **two independent, non-exclusive candidate mechanisms** for the H2S release
rather than assuming either is confirmed: (1) sulfur species intrinsic to the M2000A formulation
reacting with nitric acid, and (2) nitric acid contacting residual Ag2S-bearing "silver mud" already
present in the legacy vessel from the plant's decades of EO-catalyst processing (Sec. 2.3) — a
textbook-generic acid-plus-metal-sulfide mechanism that requires no assumption about M2000A's
composition at all.
