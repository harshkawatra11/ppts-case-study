# Lane 6 — Comparative Accident Database

Research reference material for the Ames Goldsmith / Catalyst Refiners (Institute/Nitro,
WV) fatal H2S release dossier. Scope: prior industrial accidents genuinely comparable to
the April 2026 incident — H2S fatalities, incompatible-chemical-mixing/cleaning incidents,
decommissioning-phase incidents, and brief historical touchstones for a "lessons from
history" framing section.

Context anchor: On **April 22, 2026**, at Ames Goldsmith Corporation's Catalyst Refiners
facility (silver-catalyst refining/recovery plant, also reported under "Nitro, WV")
in Institute, West Virginia, workers decommissioning a tank mixed **nitric acid** with a
proprietary cleaning agent (**"M2000A"**) into residual **silver-sulfide** process residue.
The reaction released toxic **hydrogen sulfide (H2S)** gas (some reporting also references
NOx/NO2 as a nitric-acid reaction byproduct), instantaneously overcoming workers in the
building. 2 fatalities, 30+ hospitalized. The plant was scheduled for closure in June
2026; the incident occurred during decommissioning/decontamination prep work. CSB opened
an investigation under Chairperson Steve Owens. [Chemistry World](https://www.chemistryworld.com/news/fatal-incident-at-us-catalyst-refiner-under-investigation/4023351.article), [Mountain State Spotlight](https://mountainstatespotlight.org/2026/04/22/kanawha-county-chemical-leak-institute/), [Wikipedia — 2026 Institute, WV chemical disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Institute,_West_Virginia_chemical_disaster), [WorkSafety24/7](https://www.worksafety247.com/article/csb-investigating-fatal-catalyst-refiners-hydrogen-sulfide-release), [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/22/g-s1-118416/chemical-leak-west-virginia-institute)

## 1. Hydrogen sulfide fatality incidents (industrial, general)

**PEMEX Deer Park Refinery, Deer Park, TX — October 10, 2024 (CSB-investigated, final
report issued 2026).** ~27,000 lb of pressurized H2S released during maintenance when
contract workers (Repcon Inc.) opened the wrong piping segment — an identical, mislabeled
line 5 feet from the intended empty pipe. The release traveled downwind; a second worker
(ISC Constructors), unaware a release was occurring, was fatally exposed in an adjacent
unit. 2 fatalities. CSB's final report cited **mislabeled/inadequately identified piping,
poor contractor management, and work-permit failures** — a near-exact structural analog to
Ames Goldsmith's decommissioning permit and hazard-communication failure. [CSB](https://www.csb.gov/us-chemical-safety-board-releases-investigation-report-on-fatal-hydrogen-sulfide-release-at-the-pemex-deer-park-refinery-in-texas/), [CSB final report PDF](https://www.csb.gov/assets/1/6/pemex_investigation_report_final.pdf), [Lion Technology](https://www.lion.com/lion-news/february-2026/deadly-hydrogen-sulfide-release-csb%E2%80%99s-final-report), [McFarlane Law](https://www.mcfarlanelaw.com/pemex-deer-park-hydrogen-sulfide-csb-report-2026/)

**California organic-waste recycling facility — drainage-shaft H2S fatality (CDPH/FACE
Case 11CA008).** Two brothers (ages 16 and 22) died cleaning a 10-ft drainage shaft with a
high-pressure hose flushing residual compost; the younger was overcome by H2S and fell, the
older died attempting a rescue without protection. Root causes: no hazardous-substance/
confined-space training, no atmospheric monitoring, no ventilation, no rescue plan — the
classic "would-be rescuer becomes second victim" pattern also seen at Georgia-Pacific
Naheola (below) and relevant to the Ames Goldsmith bystander/co-worker exposure pattern.
[CDPH FACE report](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CCDPHP/DEODC/OHB/FACE/CDPH%20Document%20Library/11CA008.pdf)

**Production Waste Solutions LLC, oil/gas waste site — September 2022.** An employee was
fatally exposed to H2S while skimming and suctioning sludge water and oil byproducts from a
sump pit. OSHA cited the company for six serious violations: exposing employees to
inhalation hazards, failing to train on H2S hazards, and failing to provide a quick
body-drench/eyewash station where corrosive materials were present. [DOL/OSHA](https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/osha/osha20230315-0)

**Aghorn Operating Inc., oil/gas facility near Odessa, TX — 2019.** A lone worker
responding to an alarm entered a pump house to isolate a malfunctioning pump; the pump
reactivated and released H2S-laden water into the enclosed space. The facility's H2S
monitors were not functioning and the worker was not wearing a personal detector — he died.
His wife was then also killed by H2S exposure when she entered the pump house looking for
him — a double fatality from an unprotected "second responder" entering a contaminated
space, structurally identical to the CDPH sibling case above. OSHA found the employer had
provided personal H2S detectors but had no written policy requiring their use, and workers
were untrained on lockout/tagout, which would have prevented pump re-energization.
[Security Magazine](https://www.securitymagazine.com/articles/99076-texas-oil-and-gas-company-cited-by-osha-after-employee-death)

**Quala Services tank-cleaning contractor, TX (Houston area) — repeat citations
2020/2024.** A tank-cleaning contractor was cited in June 2020 after two workers died
cleaning inside a tank; OSHA re-inspected in 2024 and found **the same hazards** still
present (8 repeat violations) — direct evidence that corrective action commonly fails to
stick without independent verification, relevant to any dossier recommendation that leans
on "updated procedures" alone as a remedy. [DOL/OSHA news release](https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/osha/osha20240708)

**General H2S fatality base rate.** Across a 6-year OSHA fatality dataset, H2S was the
second-leading toxic-atmosphere killer after carbon monoxide, with 46 recorded deaths;
the majority of H2S fatalities nationally occur in confined spaces — sewers, waste
lagoons, sludge/wastewater plants, tanks, and cesspools. [OSHA — Hydrogen Sulfide in Workplaces](https://www.osha.gov/hydrogen-sulfide/hydrogen-sulfide-workplaces), [OSHA H2S Release fact sheet PDF](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/OSHA4204.pdf)

## 2. Incompatible-chemical-mixing / cleaning incidents — the direct precedent class

This is the closest precedent category to Ames Goldsmith: an acid (or acidic cleaning
solution) contacting a sulfide-bearing material and generating H2S, almost always during a
transfer, cleaning, or maintenance operation rather than steady-state production. **CSB's
2004 Safety Bulletin "Sodium Hydrosulfide: Preventing Harm"** (No. 2003-03-B) is the
single most directly applicable reference document: it is a purpose-built compilation of
this exact hazard mode.

### 2.1 CSB Safety Bulletin: Sodium Hydrosulfide (NaHS/NaSH) — Preventing Harm (Nov 2004)

**Core finding.** From 1971–2004, CSB identified **45 NaHS-related incidents** across six
industry sectors (pulp & paper, transportation, manufacturing, leather tanning, chemical/
dye, refining), resulting in **32 fatalities, 176 injuries, 351 medical evaluations, and
at least 10 plant/community evacuations**. 36 of the 45 incidents were H2S releases; the
rest were skin/eye exposure to the caustic NaHS solution itself. Causal breakdown:
improper mixing or transfer (33%), spills (27%), mechanical failure (16%), improper
maintenance/repair (13%), unknown (11%).

**The core hazard chemistry — directly analogous to Ames Goldsmith.** NaHS (and other
sulfide-bearing substances, including the silver-sulfide residue at Ames Goldsmith)
releases large volumes of H2S when contacted by any acid:
`NaHS + Acid (H+) → Na+ + H2S`. CSB's two identified systemic management failures across
all 45 incidents were: (1) failure to identify and mitigate the hazard during process
system design/engineering, and (2) failure to manage the hazard once design/engineering
did not eliminate it. CSB explicitly recommended OSHA expand the Process Safety Management
(PSM) standard to cover reactive-hazard chemicals like NaHS, which — like the silver
sulfide/nitric acid/M2000A combination at Ames Goldsmith — falls outside PSM's listed
"highly hazardous chemicals" despite being capable of a mass-casualty reaction. [CSB Safety Bulletin PDF](https://www.csb.gov/assets/1/6/sodium_hydrosulfide_safety_bulletin_(nov_2004).pdf)

**Named case studies from the bulletin** (each is a distinct, citable CSB/EPA-documented
precedent):

- **Georgia-Pacific Naheola Mill, Pennington, AL — January 16, 2002 (2 killed, 8
  injured).** Covered in full detail below (Section 2.2) as CSB's dedicated investigation
  report — the single closest analog to Ames Goldsmith in the entire database.
- **Horween Leather Company, Chicago, IL — February 14, 1978 (8 killed, 43 injured, 57
  sought medical evaluation).** A substitute delivery driver, unfamiliar with plant
  operations and NaHS hazards, and unsupervised by plant personnel, pumped a tanker of
  NaHS into a storage tank that already contained acid. A large volume of H2S was
  immediately generated, forcing evacuation of the entire plant and surrounding community.
  The tank's fill-line coupling was designed to prevent this exact mis-connection, but the
  driver fabricated a makeshift coupling when the correct one wouldn't fit — a direct
  precedent for "safeguard defeated by workaround under time pressure."
- **Prime Tanning Company, Berwick, ME — April 2, 1971 (6 killed).** NaHS delivery
  drivers, instructed where to offload, found their tanker's fill nozzle didn't match the
  tank's coupler; they used an adapter nozzle to force the connection. The tank actually
  contained acidic chrome tanning liquor (pH 3–4) and had no labeling identifying its
  contents. ~160 gallons of NaHS were transferred before a driver noticed workers inside
  the plant collapsing. This is the earliest incident in CSB's dataset and establishes
  that "mismatched coupling defeated by an improvised adapter, no hazard labeling on the
  receiving vessel" is a 50+ year recurring failure mode.
- **Whitehall Leather Company, Whitehall, MI — June 4, 1999 (1 killed, 1 injured).** A
  night-shift tanker driver, arriving with a load of NaHS, was directed by a shift
  supervisor — who had never received a NaHS shipment and mistakenly believed it was
  "pickle acid" (hydrochloric acid) — to unload it into the pickle-acid storage tank. The
  resulting H2S release killed the driver and injured a plant employee. A precise analog
  to any scenario where personnel misidentify which chemical is present in a vessel before
  a transfer or cleaning operation.
- **Powell Duffryn terminal fire, Savannah, GA — April 10, 1995 (337 medical
  evaluations, 30+ day community evacuation of 2,000 residents).** An explosion in a
  co-located solvent storage tank fractured NaHS transfer piping; up to 300,000 gallons of
  NaHS spilled and mixed with a similarly large quantity of acidic cleaning solution inside
  a shared earthen containment berm. The enormous resulting H2S release caused 337 people
  to seek medical evaluation and forced a 30-day evacuation as H2S continued off-gassing
  from NaHS-saturated soil. CSB's takeaway — failure to identify the hazard of co-locating
  incompatible materials inside a single spill containment — is directly relevant to any
  Ames Goldsmith finding about shared containment/drainage between acid and sulfide-bearing
  process residues. [EPA investigation report, 1998](https://yosemite.epa.gov/opa/admpress.nsf/0/8e314f057b62e118852565fe006fa986?OpenDocument)
- **Stone Savannah River pulp and paper mill, Wentworth, GA — December 8, 1990 (1
  killed, 4 injured).** During routine **acid cleaning** of a 20,000-gallon pulping-solution
  pressure filter — a cleaning operation, not normal production — H2S was generated inside
  the vessel as the acid cleaning solution dissolved NaHS-containing pulping filtrate.
  Local exhaust ventilation normally controlled this, but on the incident day H2S leaked
  from the system and accumulated in the walled containment where workers were operating
  valves; they smelled it and tried to escape but were overwhelmed before they could climb
  out of the valve pit. This is CSB's most direct "cleaning operation generates H2S from
  residual sulfide chemistry" precedent — the same causal structure as Ames Goldsmith's
  tank decommissioning/cleaning.
- **Westvaco paper mill, Covington, VA — May 4, 1988 (10 injured).** A 300,000-gallon
  storage tank containing NaHS-bearing pulping solution collapsed; the material entered
  the plant sewer system (which routinely handled storm runoff, spills, and waste) and
  reacted with waste acids already present in the sewer, producing H2S that escaped
  through drains, vents, and manways, injuring 10 employees working nearby.

### 2.2 Georgia-Pacific Naheola Mill, Pennington, Alabama — January 16, 2002 (CSB Report No. 2002-01-I-AL) — the closest single precedent

CSB's dedicated investigation report on this incident is the most structurally similar
precedent to Ames Goldsmith found in this research pass, and is worth citing in depth.

**What happened.** Sodium hydrosulfide (NaSH) was being unloaded from tanker trucks (15
trucks in the preceding 24 hours) at a mill tank-truck unloading station. A shallow
containment pit ("oil pit") next to the unloading pad collected incidental NaSH spillage
(pump leaks, line flushing, hose-connection drips — typically ~5 gallons per truck). This
pit drained, via a connection made years earlier during an unrelated capital project, into
the mill's "acid sewer" — a line to which operators manually added sulfuric acid daily to
control effluent pH. On the day of the incident, the chlorine dioxide unit that normally
diluted the acid sewer's flow was offline, so the sewer's acid was more concentrated than
normal. An operator drained the oil pit (routine procedure) while, simultaneously, a
delivery driver connected a new NaSH tanker, spilling ~5 gallons at the hose connection.
The NaSH reacted with the concentrated sulfuric acid in the sewer, generating H2S within
5 minutes. The gas leaked through a gap in the seal of a nearby sewer manway — right where
Burkes Construction contractors were doing unrelated overhead-piperack maintenance work.
Two contractors were killed instantly; seven other contractors and one truck driver were
injured; six responding county paramedics later reported H2S exposure symptoms themselves
after transporting victims, because the victims were never decontaminated at the scene.

**Root causes (CSB's own language):**
1. *"Good engineering and process safety practices were not followed when joining the
   drain from the truck unloading station and the oil pit to the acid sewer."* Neither the
   chemicals that could enter the sewer, nor the hazards of their interaction, were ever
   identified — no hazard review or management-of-change (MOC) analysis was performed when
   the drain connections were made in 1995, years before the incident.
2. *"There was no management system to incorporate hazard warnings about mixing [NaSH]
   with acid into process safety information."* The manufacturer's own MSDS explicitly
   warned that NaSH generates H2S on contact with acid and recommended preventing runoff
   from entering sewers — but this information was never translated into mill operating
   procedures or worker training.

**Contributing causes:** a manway cover (originally an open grate, later fitted with a
fiberglass seal) that was never adequately designed to remain airtight — prior chlorine
dioxide leaks from the same manway had been informally repaired via work order but never
formally investigated as incidents, because Georgia-Pacific's own near-miss investigation
policy had not yet been implemented at that specific mill; and inadequate H2S hazard
training for the contractors, who had only "basic awareness" of H2S and whose escape
respirators (rated only for low-concentration atmospheres) were useless at the actual
exposure levels.

**Regulatory finding of particular relevance:** the incident area was **not covered by
OSHA's Process Safety Management standard** — H2S itself is PSM-listed, but it was not
present in threshold quantity at the location, and NaSH (the precursor) is not a PSM-listed
chemical at all despite its demonstrated reactive hazard. This is the same regulatory gap
CSB flagged in the 2004 NaHS bulletin, and it is directly relevant to Ames Goldsmith:
silver sulfide and M2000A/nitric acid are similarly unlikely to appear on a PSM highly
hazardous chemicals list even though their combination is lethal.

**CSB recommendations issued** (selected, most transferable to Ames Goldsmith): evaluate
process sewers/drains where chemicals may collect and interact and identify hazardous
reaction scenarios before connecting waste streams; identify all areas (confined *and
unconfined* — the Naheola release happened in open, flat ground, not a confined space)
where H2S could be generated and install monitors/alarms; apply hazard reviews and MOC
analysis whenever additions or changes are made to systems where incompatible chemicals
could collect; update emergency response plans to include decontamination procedures for
personnel exposed to toxic gas, specifically to protect responders from secondary
exposure (ATSDR's own Medical Management Guidelines did not, at the time, recognize that
H2S-exposed victims can off-gas and endanger the medical personnel treating them — CSB
recommended ATSDR fix this). [Full CSB Investigation Report No. 2002-01-I-AL, January 2003](https://www.csb.gov/assets/1/20/gp_report.pdf?13764=)

### 2.3 Other named incompatible-mixing incidents (non-sulfide, general precedent value)

**AB Specialty Silicones, Waukegan, IL — May 3, 2019.** Mixing of two incompatible
chemicals during a manufacturing process caused a violent explosion and fire. **4 workers
killed**, a 5th injured, the facility destroyed, neighboring businesses damaged. CSB
identified it as a textbook case of inadequate process hazard analysis around chemical
compatibility. [AIHA summary of CSB findings](https://www.aiha.org/news/220505-csb-mixture-of-incompatible-chemicals-caused-fatal-2019-explosion)

**MGPI Ingredients, Atchison, KS — October 21, 2016 (CSB case study, published Jan
2018).** During routine tank-farm delivery of sulfuric acid, incompatible materials mixed
and released a chlorine-containing toxic cloud into the surrounding community. Over 140
people (public, MGPI staff, and the delivery driver) sought medical care. CSB's case
study, *"Key Lessons for Preventing Inadvertent Mixing During Chemical Unloading
Operations,"* drew 11 lessons centered on evaluating unloading equipment/process design
and building in human-factors safeguards against misconnection/mislabeling — the same
causal category implicated at Deer Park, at Whitehall/Prime Tanning/Horween above, and per
early reporting, at Ames Goldsmith. [World Grain coverage](https://www.world-grain.com/articles/9168-csb-publishes-case-study-on-mixing-incident-at-mgpi), [CSB case study PDF](https://www.csb.gov/assets/1/20/mgpi_case_study.pdf)

**US Ink, East Rutherford, NJ (CSB case study, Jan 2015).** Additional CSB incompatible-
mixing/reactive-chemical case study in the same series; useful as a secondary citation for
the "reactive hazards during non-routine operations" pattern. [CSB case study PDF](https://www.csb.gov/assets/1/20/csb_case_study.pdf)

**Darling Ingredients, Wadesboro, NC — June 22, 2023.** A chemical explosion involving an
aluminum chloride solution killed one worker in a tank rupture attributed to an
uncontrolled chemical reaction; captured in CSB's ongoing public incident-summary
initiative (Volume One covers 26 accidental-release events across 15 states), which is
itself a useful pointer to the broader universe of under-publicized reactive-chemical
incidents beyond the fully-investigated flagship cases. [Chemical Engineering — CSB incident summaries initiative](https://www.chemengonline.com/chemical-safety-board-announces-new-information-product-on-serious-industrial-chemical-incidents/), [WGHP/FOX8 — Wadesboro plant explosion](https://myfox8.com/news/north-carolina/worked-killed-in-north-carolina-chemical-plant-explosion/)

## 3. Decommissioning-phase incidents (a distinct hazard category)

The decommissioning/decontamination phase is process-safety literature's recognized
"blind spot" — normal operating safeguards (interlocks, trained operating crews, live
instrumentation, SIS coverage) are routinely stood down or physically disconnected before
or during this phase, while hazardous residues (still-reactive chemistry, deposits, scale)
remain in vessels. The Stone Savannah River acid-cleaning fatality (Section 2.1 above) and
the Georgia-Pacific Naheola case (Section 2.2) both technically occurred during
maintenance/cleaning rather than production — reinforcing that this transition period is
where the sulfide-plus-acid hazard mode concentrates.

- **IChemE "Managing Process Safety in a Decommissioning Project"** (Hazards 29 paper) —
  a practitioner framework covering the full decommissioning lifecycle from scoping
  through physical dismantling, explicitly flagging that risk profiles change as
  engineering controls are progressively removed. [IChemE PDF](https://www.icheme.org/media/19415/hazards-29-paper-35.pdf)
- **Didcot A Power Station structural collapse, UK — February 2016.** 4 fatalities during
  demolition/decommissioning-adjacent structural work — cited in industry literature as a
  reference case for decommissioning-phase fatality risk (structural rather than chemical,
  but the same "controls are down, crew composition has changed" causal frame).
- **"Practical Experience From Risks of Decommissioning of Petrochemical Facilities"**
  (ASME J. Risk & Uncertainty Pt. B) — practitioner literature specifically on
  petrochemical decommissioning risk; citable for the general claim that decommissioning
  carries a materially different risk profile than steady-state operation. [ASME](https://asmedigitalcollection.asme.org/risk/article/8/4/044701/1146734/Practical-Experience-From-Risks-of-Decommissioning)
- **HazOp/CC newsletter — "A decommissioning tragedy, and a Gulf Coast FID with an
  interface problem"** — independent process-safety commentary specifically framing
  decommissioning-phase incidents (written in the CSB case-study tradition) as a distinct,
  under-studied category; useful for framing language. [Buttondown/HazopCC](https://buttondown.com/hazopcc/archive/a-decommissioning-tragedy-and-a-gulf-coast-fid/)
- Nuclear-sector parallel: IAEA's **"Safety Assessment for Decommissioning"** guidance and
  the *Nuclear Decommissioning Case Studies* literature (ScienceDirect) establish that even
  in the most heavily regulated decommissioning domain, decommissioning-specific HAZOPs are
  treated as a mandatory, separate hazard-identification exercise — not a carry-over from
  the operating-phase HAZOP. This directly supports a finding that Ames Goldsmith's
  decommissioning plan should have undergone its own PHA/HAZOP rather than relying on
  legacy operating procedures (exactly CSB's Georgia-Pacific finding: the sewer connection
  hazard was never assessed because no formal hazard review was ever triggered for it).
  [IAEA PDF](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/Pub1604_web.pdf)

## 5. Synthesis: what the precedent database says about Ames Goldsmith

Three patterns recur across nearly every H2S/incompatible-mixing precedent above and map
directly onto the reported facts of Ames Goldsmith:

1. **The hazard chemistry itself is not novel or obscure.** Acid-on-sulfide generating
   H2S is CSB's single best-documented reactive-hazard mode (45 incidents, 32 deaths
   tracked as of 2004 alone, in a bulletin published specifically to warn industry away
   from it). The 2002 Georgia-Pacific Naheola case is essentially the same failure
   mechanism — an acid stream and a sulfide-bearing stream brought into contact without a
   hazard review ever having been performed on that specific pathway.
2. **The failure consistently happens off the PSM standard's radar.** NaSH is not a
   PSM-listed "highly hazardous chemical" despite CSB's explicit recommendation that it
   be added; the Naheola release point was outside any PSM-covered process. Silver
   sulfide, nitric acid at sub-threshold quantities, and a proprietary cleaning agent like
   M2000A are exactly the class of substances likely to fall through the same regulatory
   gap at Ames Goldsmith.
3. **Decommissioning/cleaning operations are disproportionately represented.** Stone
   Savannah River (acid cleaning of a pulping filter), Whitehall/Prime Tanning/Horween
   (delivery/transfer misconnections, not production), and Georgia-Pacific (maintenance
   contractors near a sewer during a tank-truck unloading operation) all occurred during
   non-routine, transitional, or maintenance-adjacent work — not steady-state production —
   reinforcing the decommissioning-phase risk-category argument in Section 3.
