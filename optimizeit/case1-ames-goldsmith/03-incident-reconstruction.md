# Chapter 3 — Incident Reconstruction: Timeline, Contradiction Register, and Decision Sequence

## Confidence & Provenance

This chapter synthesizes open-source news reporting, one primary regulatory source (the U.S.
Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board, "CSB"), and directly-fetched federal records
(OSHA IMIS, EPA TRI Explorer) current through the most recent research pass (session date
2026-08-02, ~3.5 months post-incident). No CSB final report, OSHA citation, or coroner's report
has been published as of this date — the account below is a reconstruction from contemporaneous
journalism and agency press statements, not from an adjudicated investigative finding. Every
non-trivial claim is tagged **[VERIFIED]**, **[VERIFIED, search-synthesis]**, **[INFERRED]**, or
**[HYPOTHESIS]** per the dossier's epistemic-tagging convention, with a chapter-local `[S-nn]`
citation resolved in the Sources section. Numeric and factual disagreements between sources are
treated as a first-class deliverable of this chapter (the Contradiction Register), not smoothed
over into false precision.

**Location note (used once here, then "the facility" throughout):** most press coverage —
Wikipedia, Mountain State Spotlight, West Virginia Watch, WV MetroNews's initial headline, WCHS,
WV Public Broadcasting, PBS NewsHour, ABC News, Fortune, The Hill — places the incident in
**Institute, WV**. The CSB's own press release and Ames Goldsmith's own company statement both
say **Nitro, WV**. The registered street address, 1580 1st Avenue South, resolves to Nitro, WV
25143 in multiple business directories and in the OSHA IMIS record. Both names refer to the same
Catalyst Refiners plant on the Institute/Nitro line in Kanawha County; see the Contradiction
Register, Row 1, for full sourcing. [S-01][S-02][S-03]

**Casualty-count framing used throughout this dossier:** **2 fatalities** (uncontested across
every source) **[VERIFIED]**; **hospitalized/treated counts reported across at least five
distinct aggregate figures — 19, 21, 22, ~30, and "30+ sought medical care" — not reconciled by
any single source** **[VERIFIED, range not point estimate]**; **7 first-responders/EMS personnel
among the injured, well-corroborated across nearly all outlets** **[VERIFIED]**. Names of the
two deceased are **not publicly identified as of the most recent research date (2026-08-02)**.

## 3. Work-Permit and Decision-Sequence Reconstruction

### 3.1 What is [VERIFIED] as reported

- The facility was in **permanent decommissioning**, with closure scheduled for **June 2026**;
  workers had been informed of the closure months in advance. **[VERIFIED]** [S-14]
- The work being performed at the time of the release was **cleaning of a legacy processing
  vessel** as part of decommissioning/dismantling activity. **[VERIFIED]** [S-15][S-16][S-02]
- The two substances introduced into the vessel were **concentrated nitric acid (HNO3)** and a
  **proprietary cleaning product identified as "M2000A"** (Bonderite-branded; exact formulation
  match to the publicly documented "BONDERITE M-AD 2000A" alkaline corrosion-inhibited cleaner is
  unconfirmed). **[VERIFIED, product-identity caveat noted]** [S-19][S-17]
- The vessel contained **residual "silver mud"** — a sulphurous, Ag2S-bearing sediment left over
  from electrolytic silver/EO-catalyst refining. **[VERIFIED]** [S-17]
- **No named individuals, job titles, work-permit numbers, or specific internal procedural
  documents have been publicly reported** as of the most recent research date. The two deceased
  are not publicly identified. No source names a supervisor, a permit-issuing authority, or a
  specific procedure that was or was not followed. **This chapter does not invent any of the
  above** — where the case brief's framing implies a specific decision sequence, the account
  below is explicitly labeled [INFERRED] engineering reconstruction, not reported fact.

### 3.2 [INFERRED] engineering reconstruction of the likely task sequence

Reasoning chain, grounded in standard industrial decommissioning practice and the verified facts
above:

1. **Task context.** Tank/vessel cleaning during decommissioning typically follows a sequence of
   (a) draining/removing bulk process material, (b) chemical or mechanical cleaning of internal
   surfaces to remove adherent residues, and (c) inspection/confirmation before the vessel is
   certified empty, decontaminated, or cut up for scrap/disposal. Nitric acid is a standard
   reagent in silver-refining operations generally — it is used elsewhere in the same facility's
   normal process to dissolve extraneous base metal from silver-bearing feedstock — and its
   presence on-site as a decommissioning-cleaning reagent is unsurprising. **[INFERRED]**
2. **Why two chemicals were introduced together.** A plausible, non-exotic explanation consistent
   with all verified facts is that the vessel-cleaning task called for **sequential or combined
   use of an acid rinse/dissolution step (nitric acid) and a surfactant/degreasing step
   (Bonderite M2000A-type product)** intended to loosen and remove different classes of residue
   (metallic/mineral scale vs. organic or particulate film) from the vessel's interior surfaces.
   If the vessel's actual residual content — "silver mud," predominantly insoluble silver sulfide
   — was not fully characterized, verified as removed, or accounted for in the work-planning
   step before the cleaning chemicals were applied, then the combination of a strong mineral acid
   with a sulfide-bearing residue is sufficient by itself to generate the H2S release described
   in the incident, independent of whether M2000A played any direct chemical role beyond being
   present in the same confined vessel atmosphere at the time. **[INFERRED]** (Chapter 4 addresses
   the specific reaction chemistry.)
3. **Why the hazard was not anticipated.** Three verified, independent facts converge to make an
   unrecognized-hazard scenario plausible without requiring any invented narrative: (a) the
   product safety data sheet for M2000A was reportedly not available to first responders through
   facility hazard documentation and had to be located via an ad hoc internet search during the
   active response (§4 below) — indicating a documentation gap that plausibly extended to
   pre-task hazard review as well, not only emergency response; (b) the facility was in a
   decommissioning phase with an approaching closure date, a period in which staffing,
   supervisory attention, and procedural rigor at industrial sites are frequently reduced
   relative to normal operations (a general pattern noted in process-safety literature, not a
   site-specific reported fact here — see Chapter 6 for the broader precedent discussion); and
   (c) "silver mud" residues are, by their nature as legacy/aged vessel contents, less likely to
   have current characterization data than actively-processed material. **[INFERRED — a, from
   the Tier II finding directly; b and c, from general decommissioning risk patterns, not
   incident-specific reporting]**
4. **What this reconstruction does not claim.** It does not assert who authorized the task, what
   the written procedure said or did not say, whether a permit-to-work or line-breaking/
   confined-space permit was in place, whether a job hazard analysis was performed, or whose
   decision introduced the two chemicals into the vessel. None of these facts have been publicly
   reported as of 2026-08-02. Any chapter downstream that speculates further on procedural
   specifics must carry the same [INFERRED] tag and the same caveat.

### 3.3 Alternative explanations not ruled out

- **[HYPOTHESIS]** M2000A itself may have contributed a chemically active role beyond being a
  co-present surfactant (e.g., if its alkaline/oxidizing components interacted directly with
  Ag2S or with the nitric acid stream) — cannot be assessed without the actual SDS/formulation,
  which is not publicly available for the specific product cited in the incident. Chapter 4
  should treat this as an open chemistry question, not a settled mechanism.
- **[HYPOTHESIS]** The vessel may have already contained residual acid or reactive material from
  a prior process step before the cleaning task began, rather than the cleaning task itself being
  the sole point of chemical introduction — no source confirms or excludes this.

## 4. The NO2 Question — Where the Case Brief Goes Beyond Open Reporting

The case brief for this dossier states that the release consisted of "lethal concentrations of
gaseous hydrogen sulfide (H2S) alongside toxic nitrogen dioxide (NO2) fumes." Open-source
reporting reviewed for this chapter — the WV Gazette-Mail, the CSB's own press release and case
page, WV MetroNews, NPR, PBS NewsHour, Alliance Chemical's summary, business-news-today.com, the
Powell & Majestro legal summary, and Wikipedia — **consistently and exclusively characterizes the
released toxic agent as hydrogen sulfide.** No source located in this research pass independently
reports a measured NO2 concentration, an NO2-specific victim symptom, or an official statement
naming NO2 as a released agent. **[VERIFIED — absence of NO2 confirmation across all reviewed
open reporting]** [S-40]

This is a case where **the case brief's framing goes beyond what the available open-source
record supports.** It is not treated in this dossier as an error to be silently corrected —
nitrogen dioxide is a chemically plausible, even expected, co-product where concentrated nitric
acid decomposes or acts as an oxidizer under the thermal/pressure conditions implied by a
"violent reaction" inside a sealed or partially sealed vessel (the canonical decomposition
pathway is 4HNO3 → 4NO2 + O2 + 2H2O). Chapter 4 (Reaction Chemistry) evaluates this pathway on
its chemical merits and should present any NO2 formation finding as **[INFERRED]** — a reasoned
chemical expectation given the reagents and conditions — and never elevate it to the same
evidentiary footing as the [VERIFIED] H2S release, which has direct multi-source and primary-
source (CSB) confirmation. Any slide, exhibit, or downstream chapter that lists "H2S and NO2" as
co-equally confirmed released agents should be corrected to reflect this asymmetry.

## 5. The Tier II / M2000A Hazard-Communication Gap

A separately significant, well-sourced finding belongs in the incident record itself, not only
in the root-cause and regulatory chapters that will expand on it: per WV Gazette-Mail reporting
published **May 7, 2026** ("Officials were in the dark on key chemical in fatal Nitro industrial
site incident"), **Bonderite M2000A was not listed on Catalyst Refiners' federally required
EPCRA Tier II Emergency and Hazardous Chemical Inventory form.** **[VERIFIED, search-synthesis —
direct fetch of the source article returned HTTP 429 on repeated attempts; content is based on
search-engine-indexed synthesis of the article, not a verbatim primary read]** [S-35]

Per the same reporting: emergency responders and officials stated that having M2000A properly
listed on the facility's Tier II inventory would have improved their response, since they did
not know what chemical they were dealing with as the release was occurring. Responders reportedly
located the product's safety data sheet via an **approximately 20-minute Google search conducted
during the active, ongoing toxic release**, rather than retrieving it from facility hazard
documentation that EPCRA §312 Tier II reporting is specifically designed to make available to
local emergency planning committees and responders in advance.

This is an incident fact with direct operational consequences — the interval during which
responders operated without confirmed knowledge of one of the two reactive chemicals involved is
a documented, reported feature of the emergency-response phase of this event, not merely a
downstream regulatory-compliance footnote. It is flagged here at the incident-reconstruction
level because it plausibly affected real-time triage, PPE selection, and decontamination protocol
decisions during the response window described in §1.2–1.3 above, and because — per the reasoning
in §3.2 — a Tier II omission for a product used in an active cleaning task raises the [INFERRED]
possibility that the same documentation gap extended upstream into pre-task hazard review, not
only into post-release emergency response. Chapters 5 (Root Cause) and 7 (Regulatory Gap
Analysis) should treat this finding as a load-bearing fact and are expected to reference it by
name.
