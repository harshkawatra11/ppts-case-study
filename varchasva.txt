  District health systems in India run on delayed, fragmented information. A    
  Primary Health Centre in Rajasthan can run out of ORS during a heatwave while 
  a Community Health Centre twenty minutes away sits on surplus stock, and      
  nobody at the district office finds out until a patient is turned away.       
                                                                                
  This is not primarily a scarcity problem. Rural Health Statistics data shows  
  31,053 Primary Health Centres serve rural and semi-urban India, and most      
  still report facility status through periodic manual returns rather than live 
  data. Specialist vacancy at Community Health Centres nationally stands at     
  79.5 percent, and male health worker posts at PHCs and sub-centres run 41.9   
  percent vacant.                                                               
                                                                                
  These numbers point to the same failure from different angles. Facilities     
  generate operational signals every day: medicine stock levels, bed occupancy, 
  doctor attendance, diagnostic availability, and patient footfall. Nothing     
  connects those signals into a district-level picture before a shortage        
  becomes visible to a patient standing at an empty counter.                    
                                                                                
  A district administrator overseeing a dozen facilities has no way to see      
  which one needs attention first, which nearby facility could help, or why a   
  risk score changed overnight. The result is that health administrators make   
  daily decisions almost entirely in reactive mode, discovering problems only   
  after they have already affected care.                                        
                                                                                
  Solution                                                                      
                                                                                
  SwasthyaGrid is an AI-assisted district health operations concept that turns  
  scattered facility data into an early warning and coordination system, built  
  around one rule: the AI proposes and explains, a human administrator decides. 
                                                                                
  Each facility reports daily operational data: medicine stock, bed occupancy,  
  doctor attendance, diagnostic availability, and patient footfall. A           
  forecasting layer converts this into concrete risk signals using transparent, 
  auditable formulas. For example, days of medicine remaining is calculated as  
  units remaining divided by average daily consumption, with risk classified    
  as high below three days and medium below six.                                
                                                                                
  When a facility is flagged at risk, a recommendation engine searches nearby   
  facilities for surplus capacity, ranks candidates by distance and available   
  quantity, and calculates a specific transfer amount, whether that is a stock  
  transfer, a bed redirect, a staff transfer, or a diagnostic redirect. Every   
  recommendation carries a confidence score built from the forecast strength,   
  supporting factors, logistics distance, and safety margin, so the             
  administrator sees not just what is recommended but why.                      
                                                                                
  Gemini is used strictly as an explanation and question-answering layer that   
  reads from these already-computed backend results. It never generates or      
  guesses numbers on its own. Every recommendation is created in a pending      
  state and stays there until a human administrator approves, rejects, or       
  modifies it, so no action is ever taken automatically.                        
                                                                                
  A separate citizen-facing layer, kept fully isolated from internal district   
  operations data, gives the public emergency first aid guidance and helps      
  locate the nearest functioning PHC or hospital, always directing              
  life-threatening situations to call 108.                                      
                                                                                
  The goal is to help district health teams move from finding out about a       
  crisis after it happens to seeing it coming while there is still time to act, 
  without ever removing human judgment from the decision.
