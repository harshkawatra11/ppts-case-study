## 9. Where the DTU engine leaks: five breaks between a student who has heard of BeyondTech and one who enrols

**Answer.** Recall of BeyondTech at DTU is genuinely strong (B11), so the problem is never awareness; it is five distinct breaks between hearing about the programme and paying for it, each with its own mechanism, and each avoidable without spending on ads. Three of the five have a hard number behind them (founder-hours load, an open-link funnel with no capture, and a near-zero second-year share); two are structural claims that this research can describe but cannot size from outside the company, and the doc says so rather than inventing a figure. Together they explain why 80% of today's enrolments still arrive through one channel (referral, A05) eight years into the product's life, and why organic-first, earlier-entry fixes outperform a paid-awareness campaign for this specific funnel.

### 9.1 The issue tree (MECE)

```
Student has heard of BeyondTech, has not enrolled
├── 1. Referral reaches the wrong students
│     Only circuital-branch fourth years sit inside the word-of-mouth chain (I1)
├── 2. The top of the funnel has no net
│     8 free PDFs and the course sampler are open links with no capture (I2, W07, W08)
├── 3. The sales motion is one person, one chat, every time
│     1:1 WhatsApp is the only contact channel; it does not scale with demand (W11)
├── 4. The admission gate adds friction and founder time
│     Application, review, fit interview, then payment; no price shown anywhere (W06)
└── 5. There is no mechanism built for second or third years at all
      Today's acquisition skews almost entirely to students already close to placement season (A28 baseline)
```

These five exhaust the funnel from first contact to payment (MECE: every stage between "has heard of" and "has paid" is covered exactly once) and each is evidenced below.

| # | Leak | Mechanism | Number or finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Referral reaches the wrong students | Word of mouth travels through placed seniors, concentrating in circuital branches | Not measurable from outside: branch-wise intake split not found [RB gap 1]; branch-wise placement gap confirmed (section 8.2) |
| 2 | Top of the funnel has no net | 8 PDFs and the sampler are open, ungated links | 8 of 8 links confirmed genuinely open [RA21, W08]; A26 models a 20% to 50% capture rate if gated (base 35%) |
| 3 | Sales motion is 1:1 and does not scale | WhatsApp chat, one phone number, founder as sole operator | 220 founder-hours a year at 110 enrolments today (2.0 hours each, A22) [M T11] |
| 4 | Admission gate adds friction and founder time | Application, review, fit interview, payment; no price shown | Not measurable from outside: no applicant-level drop-off data exists; hypothesis I10 only |
| 5 | No second- or third-year capture mechanism | Acquisition concentrates on students already near a placement decision | Second-year share at baseline: 0.0% [M T13]; target after the plan: 20% (A28 base) |

### 9.2 Leak 1: referral reaches circuital-branch fourth years and skips the rest

Branch-wise placement outcomes confirm a real circuital-versus-non-circuital gap at DTU: Computer Engineering, Software Engineering, IT and ECE all post average packages from ₹15.82 to ₹19.85 LPA against ₹9.20 to ₹12.89 LPA for Civil, Mechanical and Electrical [RB17, RB22, RB23]. A referral chain that runs through placed seniors naturally concentrates inside the branches where seniors are being placed into non-tech roles most visibly and most often discussed, which the placement data suggests skews circuital. This is the one leak where this research cannot supply a confirmed percentage: DTU's branch-wise intake split was not found [RB gap 1], so the exact share of BeyondTech's current base that is circuital versus non-circuital is not measurable from outside the company; what is measurable is the referral-share figure itself (80% of enrolments today, A05) and the branch-level placement gap that referral would plausibly inherit. The doc states this as a well-evidenced mechanism with an unconfirmed exact share, not as a precise statistic.

### 9.3 Leak 2: ungated lead magnets lose the lead at the top, now confirmed genuinely open

BeyondTech's resources page carries exactly 8 free PDFs (Online Assessments Guide, Bain Analyst Roadmap, Meesho Business Analyst Roadmap, Product Analyst Roadmap, Business Analyst Roadmap, Data Analyst Roadmap, Management Consulting Roadmap, and a Projects Guide for Non-Tech Profiles), each served as an open Google Drive link [RA21, confirming W08]. A direct check of each link's page content, not just its HTTP status, found a populated public-preview meta tag on all 8 with no "request access" wall, confirming genuinely open access, not merely unconfirmed access [conflicts.md, "Confirmed, no action needed"]. The Guesstimate module of the course sampler is free to watch with the same no-capture pattern [W07]. Every one of these touchpoints is a moment where a visitor who is interested enough to click a PDF link leaves no trace: no email, no WhatsApp number, no name. Assumption A26 models what a gating change would recover, a 20% to 50% capture rate on this traffic (bear to bull, base 35%) [A26], but this assumption is flagged explicitly in the assumptions register as low confidence, since no comparable bootcamp-specific or India-specific benchmark survives verification [conflicts.md, F-C5]. Monthly organic visitor volume to the resources page (A27, 300 to 1,200 a month, base 600) carries the same low-confidence flag [A27, conflicts.md F-C5]. These two numbers size the opportunity honestly as a range with an explicit confidence caveat, not as a precise forecast.

| Assumption | Bear | Base | Bull | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A26, gated-content capture rate after the fix | 20% | 35% | 50% | Low; no comparable bootcamp-specific or India-specific benchmark found [conflicts.md F-C5] |
| A27, monthly organic visitors to the resources page | 300 | 600 | 1,200 | Low; same flag [conflicts.md F-C5] |

### 9.4 Leak 3: the 1:1 WhatsApp sales motion does not scale

Every contact path on BeyondTech's own site resolves to the same mechanism: a WhatsApp chat with a prefilled message ("Hi, I'm interested in BeyondTech services. Can you help me get started?") routed to a single phone number [RA25, confirming W11]. The "Request a callback" CTA, which a corrected reading places on the placement-mastery page rather than the contact page (A-C2), leads to the same personal channel. This is a sales motion with exactly one operator: the founder himself. The model's founder-hours figures make the scaling limit explicit: at today's volume, each enrolment costs 2.0 founder-hours on average (bear 3.0, bull 1.5) [A22], for a total of 220 founder-hours a year at 110 enrolments [M T11]. Every additional enrolment through this same motion, with no change to the process, adds hours linearly; the model's 24-hour net saving after the plan (section 9.6 below) comes specifically from replacing part of this 1:1 load with group formats, not from the motion scaling on its own.

### 9.5 Leak 4: the gated fit-interview application adds friction and founder time

Admission runs through four steps: submit an application, application review, a "culture and fit" interview that explicitly tests "readiness to pay the enrollment fee if selected," and only then payment and enrolment, with no price shown anywhere on the site [RA17, confirming W06]. Two separate costs sit inside this single leak. First, a human-judged step (the fit interview) sits between every interested student and every enrolled one, which is a founder-hours cost by construction, the same cost quantified in section 9.4. Second, price opacity combined with a gate that explicitly screens for "readiness to pay" may be suppressing applications from students who assume the programme costs more than it does, or who are unwilling to commit time to an interview before knowing the number; this is stated in the plan as insight I10, a hypothesis with no primary data behind it, never as a confirmed finding, because this research is desk-only and no applicant survey exists to test it.

### 9.6 Leak 5: no second- or third-year capture mechanism exists at all

The model's KPI-target table shows a second-year share of DTU acquisitions at 0.0% at baseline [M T13], and assumption A28 sets a post-plan target of 20% second-year share in the base case (10% bear, 30% bull) [A28]. A target this far above a measured zero implies, rather than directly proves, that today's acquisition is concentrated in third and fourth years close to a placement decision; no society workshop, mock drive or structured outreach aimed specifically at second years exists in any touchpoint this research found on BeyondTech's own site or its public channels. This is the structural mirror of leak 1: referral both skips non-circuital branches and skips earlier years, for the same underlying reason (word of mouth travels fastest among peers who are already deep in the same placement conversation).

### 9.7 Founder hours: the cost of running the funnel as it stands, and what the plan changes

| Metric | Hours a year |
|---|---|
| Today (110 enrolments x 2.0 hours each, A22 base) | 220 |
| After the plan, DTU alone (target enrolments x 0.8 hours each, A23 base) | 132 |
| Added by the four new campuses combined | 64 |
| After the plan, total across 5 campuses | 196 |
| Hours freed at DTU specifically | 88 |
| Founder's annual capacity (A31, 20 hours a week base x 52) | 1,040 |

[M T11]. The plan reduces total founder hours by 24 a year (220 to 196) despite expanding from one campus to five, because the hours-per-enrolment rate at DTU falls from 2.0 to 0.8 (A22 to A23), driven by replacing part of the 1:1 WhatsApp-and-interview motion with group information sessions, alumni-run interviews and a published price band [A23 basis]. At 196 hours against a 1,040-hour annual capacity, the plan fits comfortably inside one founder's available time even before counting any delegation [M T11, "fits in founder's week": True]. This is the single clearest answer this research can give to playbook rule P11 (state the founder's time cost, never overpromise it away).

| Scenario | Hours per enrolment today (A22) | Hours per enrolment after the plan (A23) |
|---|---|---|
| Bear | 3.0 | 1.2 |
| Base | 2.0 | 0.8 |
| Bull | 1.5 | 0.5 |

[A22, A23]. The hours-per-enrolment rate falls in every scenario, not only the base case, which is why the founder-hours saving in section 12.6 holds across the bear-to-bull range, not as a base-case artefact alone. The bear scenario, where each enrolment costs 3.0 founder-hours today against 1.2 hours after the plan, is the scenario with the largest proportional saving (60%), precisely because the bear case is also the scenario where the fit interview and the WhatsApp back-and-forth take longest per student, since a less-converting funnel means more unresolved conversations sitting in the founder's queue at any one time. This is a second, independent reading of why leak 3 and leak 4 compound each other: a slower-converting top of funnel does not just produce fewer enrolments, it produces more founder-hours spent per enrolment that does land, which is exactly the relationship the model's A22 bear/base/bull bracket (3.0/2.0/1.5) encodes.

### 11.6 Why the layer order matters: each layer closes a leak before the next one spends

The three-layer structure in this section is not a cosmetic grouping; each layer is sequenced specifically against the five leaks identified in section 9, so that money and founder-hours are never spent widening a funnel that still has a hole in it.

Layer 1 (capture) comes first because leaks 2 and 4, the ungated library and the opaque price band, both sit at the very top and the very bottom of the funnel respectively, and both can be fixed at zero cash cost before a single rupee is spent on reach. Gating the library specifically changes the economics of every channel that follows: C-ORG-4's modelled CAC of ₹126, the cheapest in the whole plan, only holds once the capture form exists, and every paid retargeting audience in layer 3 depends on having a pool of people who filled that form, downloaded a roadmap, or registered for a workshop, to retarget in the first place. A campaign that skipped layer 1 and went straight to layer 3's retargeting (C-PD-1) would have nothing to retarget against.

Layer 2 (organic distribution) is sequenced second because it is where leak 1 (referral skipping non-circuital branches, section 9.2) and leak 5 (no second- or third-year mechanism, section 9.6) actually get fixed, not merely described. Department and branch representatives (C-ORG-2) are the direct answer to leak 1: a representative placed in Mechanical, Civil, Electrical and Biotechnology reaches students the circuital-branch referral chain structurally does not. The mock placement drive (C-ORG-3) and the society co-hosted workshops (C-ORG-1) are the direct answer to leak 5, since both can be explicitly scheduled and marketed toward second and third years rather than left to arrive organically, the way referral currently skews toward fourth years closer to a placement decision. The structured referral channel (C-ORG-5) deliberately does not try to replace the 80% of volume that already arrives informally (A05); it formalises and rewards it, which is a different job from the other six organic channels, each of which is aimed at a segment referral does not currently reach.

Layer 3 (paid amplification) is sequenced last and is explicitly ordered within itself by how warm the audience is: retargeting (C-PD-1) before prospecting (C-PD-2), because a warm audience that has already shown interest through layers 1 and 2 converts at a fraction of the cost of a cold campus-wide Meta or LinkedIn audience that has touched nothing yet. This ordering is the mechanical reason the budget's funding rule in section 12.1 leaves two paid channels at ₹0: once layers 1 and 2 are doing their job, the warm pool available for C-PD-1 to retarget is large enough, and cheap enough per enrolment, that the two colder paid channels are simply never the most efficient next rupee to spend. The plan's much-discussed "organic-first" posture, in other words, is not a slogan layered on top of the budget; it is the direct output of sequencing capture before distribution before amplification, exactly as this section's three layers are ordered. This also explains why the plan never asks the founder to choose between organic and paid as competing philosophies: the two are sequential stages of one funnel, where the paid layer's entire job is to extend the reach of whatever the organic layer has already warmed up, not to replace it or compete with it for the same student's attention at the same moment.

The practical consequence for anyone executing this plan month to month is a simple rule of thumb: never switch on a paid channel before the organic channel it depends on has run at least once. Meta retargeting (C-PD-1) needs a workshop registrant list, a gated-PDF download list or a mock-drive attendee list to retarget against; turning it on in October, before any organic activation has happened, would have nothing to retarget and would effectively buy cold prospecting at a warm-audience price, defeating the entire logic that makes this layer cheap. The month-by-month calendar in section 11.5 already encodes this ordering implicitly, since every month with meaningful paid spend sits after at least one organic activation in the same or preceding month, but it is worth stating as an explicit execution rule rather than leaving it to be inferred from the spreadsheet alone.

[M T6]. **Interpretive flag for the bear scenario.** The bear case's ₹19,84,811 spend figure must never stand unexplained: it is not a smaller, more conservative version of the base plan, it is what happens when several funnel-stage conversion rates (event registrant to lead, lead to application, application to enrolment) all land at their worst simultaneously. Because each stage multiplies the shortfall from the one before it, hitting even a reduced 120-enrolment target under these compounded-bad conversion rates costs nearly 8 times more per head than the base case (₹16,540 blended CAC against ₹2,131, almost an 8x jump) and produces a negative contribution of −₹14,56,811. The bull scenario's organic share of 77.1% in the opposite direction is equally worth reading correctly: it is not evidence that paid spend "does not work," it is the arithmetic result of nearly every channel converting at its best simultaneously, which lets the model satisfy a 210-enrolment target almost entirely from the cheap organic channels and the cheapest paid channel, leaving the two expensive paid channels unfunded by an even wider margin than in the base case.

### 12.7 Realism check against BeyondTech's own scale

BeyondTech's company LinkedIn page lists it as a 2-to-10-employee company with 14 people associated [W12], and its placement-mastery page itself states an average outcome salary of ₹15 LPA and a highest of ₹60 LPA [RA12], figures that, per the ruling on conflict X2, are not the ones used on DTU-specific slides but do confirm the company operates at founder-led, pre-institutional scale.

| Reference point | Figure | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| BeyondTech company size | 2 to 10 employees, 14 people associated [W12] | A founder-led company, not an institutional marketer |
| Base-case DTU budget | ₹3,51,650 [M T6] | The scale this realism check is tested against |
| A single DTU branch-wise average package, upper end | ₹19.85 LPA (Computer Engineering) [RB17] | The base-case budget is well under half of one mid-career hire's annual salary at the companies BeyondTech preps for |
| Bear-case stress spend | ₹19,84,811 [M T6] | Roughly 5.6 times the base-case budget; not a figure a 2-to-10-employee company could fund from its own cash |

A ₹3,51,650 base-case DTU budget is proportionate to a company of this size: it is well under half of even a single mid-career non-tech hire's annual salary at the companies BeyondTech itself preps students for (RB17's branch-wise average packages run from ₹9.20 to ₹19.85 LPA), and it is funded almost entirely from channels that cost cash, not headcount, which matters for a company with 2 to 10 employees and one founder running the sales motion personally (section 9.4). The bear scenario's ₹19,84,811 stress figure, by contrast, would be entirely unrealistic for a company at this scale to fund from its own cash, which is a second, independent reason (beyond the interpretive flag in section 12.3) that the bear case functions as a kill-threshold trigger rather than a spending plan: a company this size could not absorb that spend even if it wanted to.

This realism check is also why the budget's heavy reliance on a single paid channel (C-PD-1, Meta retargeting, at ₹2,67,405, over 76% of total cash) is not itself a concentration risk in the way it might look on the T4 table alone. For a company this size, a single channel that is simple to switch on, pause or scale (a Meta ad account, not a hired media-buying team) is operationally easier to manage than eleven channels drawing roughly equal budget, even though the channel table in section 11.4 lists eleven distinct activities. The founder-hours figures in the table above make the same point from a different angle: the plan does not ask BeyondTech to hire into the gap, it asks the founder to spend fewer hours per enrolment than today while the cash, not additional headcount, does the work of reaching students the current 1:1 motion cannot.

Read together, sections 8 through 12 make one connected argument: DTU's own data shows a real, branch-shaped gap in who BeyondTech currently reaches (section 8); that gap is explained by five specific, mostly fixable leaks rather than any shortage of recall (section 9); the society map offers a small number of genuinely usable partners to fix the earliest leaks cheaply, even though the research behind that map fell short of its own target (section 10); the eleven-channel plan sequences fixes before reach before paid amplification, in that order, for a reason tied directly to the leaks (section 11); and the budget that funds all of it is an output of a mechanical, cheapest-first rule applied to real, modelled channel economics, not a number chosen to sound reasonable (this section). No step in that chain depends on an invented figure, and every step that could not be sourced says so plainly rather than filling the gap with something that merely looks complete.
