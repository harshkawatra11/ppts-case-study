## 17. Trust from zero: borrow it, seed it, earn it, then hand it over

**Answer.** BeyondTech cannot buy trust at a new campus with a media budget, because its own ambassador-recruitment post drew only about 15 reactions against 269 for a Founder's Office internship post on the same page within weeks of each other [W13, W14]. The fix is a four-step ladder, not a single tactic: borrow a society's standing reputation through a co-branded case league, seed a small founding cohort at a steep discount, earn local proof once that cohort's offers land, and only then hand the role of ambassador to members of that same cohort. Each step rests on a named mechanism from the diffusion and persuasion literature, not on a hunch, and the sequence is deliberately ordered so that nothing is asked of a stranger before that stranger has seen a result.

### 17.1 The four-step ladder, mechanism by mechanism

| Step | What BeyondTech does | Named mechanism | Why this mechanism, specifically | Evidence this already works for someone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Borrow | Co-host a single case league with the campus's own consulting, finance or product society, under that society's name and its existing social accounts | Cialdini's social proof: people look to others, especially others they already see as similar or knowledgeable, to decide what is correct under uncertainty [RG54] | A new-to-campus brand has no observable track record a student can check; a society's own members and its own channel supply that check for free | BeyondTech itself borrowed DCG's standing to run this exact competition, drawing 49 reactions and 19 comments on the announcement post [W15], proof that the mechanism already converts attention for BeyondTech specifically |
| 2. Seed | Enrol a small, heavily discounted founding cohort (A17: 12 bear, 20 base, 30 bull students; A18: 30 to 50 percent discount off the standard fee) | Moore's beachhead strategy: pick one narrow, winnable segment, dominate it completely, then expand outward from that proven base, rather than attacking a whole market at once [RG53] | A campus of several thousand students is too large a target for a first push; a cohort of 12 to 30 is small enough that BeyondTech's existing mentor capacity and fit-interview process (W06) can actually serve it well | Rogers' adopter categories put "innovators" at about 2.5 percent and "early adopters" at about 13.5 percent of any population [RG51]; a discounted founding cohort is explicitly recruiting from that early tail, not waiting for the 34 percent early majority to arrive on its own |
| 3. Earn | Let the founding cohort's own placement-season offers become the proof, captured as consented, named result stories, the same device BeyondTech already runs at DTU (five named testimonials: Ayush Pratap Singh at Meesho, Suhani Sharma at Bain & Company, Ravi at OYO, Soumya Raj at ExxonMobil, Vidhi Balyan at ZS, per the corrected count [A-C6]) | Rogers' trialability and observability attributes, two of the five factors that determine how fast an innovation spreads: an outcome other students can actually see spreads faster than one they are only told about [RG52] | A discount buys a first cohort's attention, but only a real, named, verifiable offer converts the next, more skeptical tier of students | DTU's own five testimonials are the working proof that this device, once it exists, keeps producing enrolments without further paid spend |
| 4. Hand over | Recruit ambassadors only from the founding cohort, after its members have their own offers, never by a cold, unaffiliated call for ambassadors | Granovetter's strength-of-weak-ties finding: infrequent-contact acquaintances carry more novel information across otherwise separate groups than close, strong ties do, because strong-tie networks overlap too heavily with themselves [RG56, RG57] | A founding-cohort member who just placed is, to the next tier of students on that campus, exactly the kind of weak but credible tie that carries new information past a social group's own boundary; a stranger who signed up to be a brand's unpaid promoter is not | The ambassador-recruitment post that drew only 15 reactions asked students to take on a brand-service role with no personal result behind it [W13]; the job-upside post on the same page, three weeks later, drew 269 [W14], showing students respond to demonstrated personal upside, which only step 3 supplies |

Insight I4 (the 15-versus-269 reaction gap) is the single most load-bearing fact in this section. It is not a comment on BeyondTech's content quality; it is evidence that an ambassador role pitched before any proof exists is competing, in a student's attention, against every other unpaid "help us grow" ask on their feed, and loses. An ambassador role pitched after a founding cohort's own offers land is competing against nothing, because by then the ambassador candidate already has a personal result to describe, and the role is simply asking them to keep telling a true story they would plausibly tell anyway.

### 17.2 Step 1 in detail: the co-branded case league

The case league is not a new invention. BeyondTech is already running one, with DCG, for a stated prize pool of ₹40,000-plus [W15]. The new-campus version of this device uses the same format against a lower, scenario-based cost, because a new campus starts with no existing relationship to amortise the cost of discovery against:

| Scenario | Case-league cost per new campus | Basis | Comparison to BeyondTech's own DTU benchmark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bear | ₹60,000 | A21; highest of the three because no economy of scale exists yet on a first-time campus, and a larger prize is needed to pull interest without any borrowed local trust to lean on | 50 percent above the ₹40,000-plus floor BeyondTech itself set at DTU [W15], reflecting the added cost of entering cold |
| Base | ₹45,000 | A21 | About 12.5 percent above the DTU benchmark, the expected case once a society co-host is actually on board and sharing the promotional load |
| Bull | ₹30,000 | A21; lowest, once the society relationship is doing most of the convincing and the prize only needs to clear the bar of a credible, worthwhile competition | 25 percent below the DTU benchmark, achievable once a repeat relationship with the society lowers the marketing lift the prize pool has to carry alone |

The design deliberately mirrors what BeyondTech already knows works (a society-hosted, prize-anchored competition) rather than inventing an unproven format for a market the founder has never sold into before. This is consistent with the organiser playbook's own observation that a founder's real competitor on this kind of question is "the founder and an AI tool on a Sunday afternoon" [P10]: the asset here is not a clever new idea but a costed, working format applied one campus at a time.

### 17.3 Step 2 in detail: the founding cohort offer

| Scenario | Founding cohort size | Discount | Season-1 fee charged per student | What this buys |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bear | 12 students [A17] | 50 percent [A18] | ₹4,000 (half of the base A01 fee of ₹8,000 under the bear price assumption, per M T10) | A cohort small enough that the founder's own fit-interview and mentoring capacity, already stretched at DTU (T11), can serve it without adding founder hours beyond the campus's allotted 16-hour share |
| Base | 20 students [A17] | 40 percent [A18] | ₹7,200 | A cohort large enough to produce a credible spread of outcomes across more than one recruiter and role, which matters for the earn step below |
| Bull | 30 students [A17] | 30 percent [A18] | ₹12,600 | The upper bound of what one mock-interview panel can realistically process in a single season, per the plan's own basis for A17 |

The discount is a trust purchase, not a margin decision [A18 basis]. It exists to compensate the founding cohort for taking a risk BeyondTech's existing DTU students did not have to take: enrolling in a programme with no local track record, on a campus where no one has yet seen a BeyondTech student land an offer.

### 17.4 Step 3 in detail: earning proof, honestly

The founding cohort's selection rate into non-tech offers (A19) is deliberately modelled below DTU's own implied rate (about 45 percent at base, A29), at 25 percent bear, 40 percent base and 55 percent bull, because the playbook is new on that campus and has not yet had a season to prove itself [A19 basis]. This matters for how result stories are built: the doc never claims a new campus's founding cohort will match DTU's own selection economics in its first season, and the result stories captured from whichever students do place are presented as exactly what they are, consented, named, and real, never inflated by implying a cohort-wide outcome the smaller actual placement number does not support. This follows the same content-integrity rule that governs every other number in this document: a claim is never dressed in more precision or more certainty than its evidence supports.

Each of the four locked picks already has a confirmed society or cell through which a result story, once it exists, could be told back to the next cohort of applicants:

| Campus | Confirmed society or cell to co-host the proof loop | Its own reach, where confirmed |
|---|---|---|
| BITS Pilani (Pilani campus) | 180 Degrees Consulting BITS Pilani, the first undergraduate institution in India to join the 180 Degrees family [RE20] | No Instagram or LinkedIn follower count was independently confirmable for 180DC BITS Pilani after three distinct searches [RE22]; this is a genuine gap, flagged rather than estimated |
| NIT Tiruchirappalli | The Business, Finance & Consulting Club umbrella, grouping 180DC NIT Trichy and ProfNITT [RE06] | 180DC NIT Trichy, 2,902 followers on a direct fetch [RE09]; ProfNITT, 2,004 followers [RE07]; the Entrepreneurship Cell, 4,359 followers [RE14]; summed confirmed reach across these three accounts is 9,265 [RE15] |
| PEC Chandigarh | The Entrepreneurship and Incubation Cell (EIC), PEC's only confirmed, currently active non-tech society [RE14, RE20] | No follower count confirmed for the EIC's own channels after three searches [RE19]; a genuine gap |
| COEP Technological University, Pune | The COEP Consulting Club, founded explicitly to expose students to consulting careers [RE10] | No Instagram handle or follower count confirmed after three distinct searches [RE11]; a genuine gap |

The gaps in the right-hand column are reported plainly rather than papered over with an assumed number, consistent with PLAN.md's rule that a figure the team looked for and did not find is logged, not invented.

### 17.5 Step 4 in detail: handing the role over, and what it should not look like

The ambassador role is handed only to a member of the founding cohort who already has a personal result (an offer, or at minimum a completed programme with a named outcome). The structure that makes this a ladder rather than a leap is simple: BeyondTech does not advertise for ambassadors on a new campus until the first founding cohort's placement season has produced at least one consented story, because advertising for an unpaid promoter before that point reproduces the exact failure already observed at DTU (W13's 15 reactions). Section 19 of this document details the incentive ladder and selection mechanics once that condition is met; this section's job is only to state why the order matters.

### 17.6 Third-party validation of the underlying mechanism

Two named, dated, independently verified quotes support the claim that trust of this kind has to be built through people before it can be bought through advertising. A deliberate search for a third or fourth such quote, across five further distinct queries, did not surface one that was both attributable to a named, real individual with a title and date, and independently fetched rather than resting on an unverifiable paraphrase [RG63, RG64, and the Gaps note in R-G-precedents.md]. The mechanism therefore stands on these two quotes plus the four citations above (Rogers, Moore, Cialdini, Granovetter), which is stated here honestly rather than padded with a weaker third quote:

> "So, when a mother tells another mother of experience, that has the most trust." (Ghazal Alagh, co-founder, Mamaearth, speaking on Tanmay Bhat's "The NextLevel Podcast", reported by Storyboard18, 27 Feb 2024) [RG63]

> "You don't look at an ad and decide to invest in stocks... It's always a friend or family member who influences you." (Nithin Kamath, co-founder and CEO, Zerodha, in an interview with the Economic Times, quoted in Business Today, 20 Jan 2025) [RG64]

Neither quote is campus-specific; both are India-based founder statements about why word-of-mouth and trust have to precede, or substitute for, paid advertising in a consumer decision. That is the closest verifiable evidence this project could find to the specific "local trust anchor before paid acquisition" claim the ladder in this section depends on, and it is presented here as exactly that: strong supporting evidence for a general principle, not a campus-specific case study.

### 17.7 What skipping a step costs

The ladder is ordered the way it is because each step depends on the one before it having actually happened, not because a four-step sequence looks more thorough than a two-step one. The table below states, plainly, what the project's own evidence says happens if a step is skipped:

| Step skipped | What happens instead | Evidence this project already has |
|---|---|---|
| Borrow | BeyondTech enters a campus under its own, unfamiliar name, competing for attention against every other unknown brand a student sees that week | BeyondTech's own case-league announcement, run under DCG's co-branding, drew 49 reactions and 19 comments [W15]; there is no comparable BeyondTech-alone post to compare it against, which is itself the point: BeyondTech has not tried entering a new, unfamiliar audience without a co-host's name attached |
| Seed | BeyondTech sells its standard-fee programme on day one of a new campus, with no discount and no small, manageable first cohort | The founding-cohort discount (A18) exists specifically because a new campus's students are being asked to pay for an unproven local track record; removing the discount removes the only thing compensating them for that risk |
| Earn | Ambassadors or paid media are deployed before any local student has a confirmed outcome to point to | This is exactly the sequence BeyondTech already tried once, with the ambassador post drawing 15 reactions against a job post's 269 on the same channel [W13, W14] |
| Hand over | BeyondTech keeps running the founder-led, 1:1 WhatsApp sales motion (W11) at every new campus indefinitely, rather than transferring any of that load to a local, proven voice | This is the exact bottleneck named in the brief itself (B11, B13): an engine "tied to the founder's presence" that "cannot be forecast"; skipping the hand-over step reproduces the same single-point dependency at every new campus instead of fixing it once |

### 17.8 What the ladder replaces, and why that matters for the scoring

The organisers' own rubric rewards a pan-India launch plan that shows "entry without a local founder or word-of-mouth" (B18) and that is backed by numbers, not slogans (B17). The obvious answer to that brief line is to recruit ambassadors and run ads on day one. Section 4.3 of this document's earlier part already named why that answer falls short specifically for BeyondTech: the one data point it already has (W13 against W14) shows an ambassador ask with no proof behind it is roughly 18 times weaker, by engagement, than a job-upside ask on the same channel. The four-step ladder in this section is the corrected answer: it spends the case-league budget (A21) and the founding-cohort discount (A18) exactly where BeyondTech's own evidence says attention actually converts, and defers the ambassador role to the one point in the sequence where a candidate finally has something true and personal to say.

## 18. The 90-day launch playbook for one campus

**Answer.** Each new campus follows the same three-phase, 90-day structure (days 1 to 30: borrow the society's reach and set the case-league date; days 31 to 60: run the league and open founding-cohort applications; days 61 to 90: close the cohort, run fit interviews and bank the first offers), but the calendar date each phase lands on is set by that campus's own confirmed entry wedge, not by a single shared launch month. BITS Pilani's window sits in late April to early May, ahead of its Practice School preference-form cycle; NIT Tiruchirappalli's sits ahead of February's Pragyan or September's Festember; PEC Chandigarh's sits in its March E-Summit season or just after its November internship-registration opening; COEP's calendar fit remains an estimate because no confirmed campus-wide placement start date was found for it. The three kill-or-scale checkpoints inside this 90-day window (day 30, day 60, day 90) use T14's thresholds verbatim, so a campus that is clearly not converting is identified inside one quarter, not after a wasted season.

### 18.1 The three phases, task by task, owner by owner

| Phase | Days | Core tasks | Owner | Kit needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1: borrow | 1 to 30 | Identify and approach the named society role (not a named individual, since office-bearers turn over); confirm a co-branded case-league date against that campus's own entry wedge; finalise the case-league prize pool and format; begin building the campus-specific kit | Team IdeaForge (Harsh Kawatra) owns outreach and the application tracker; the society's own office-bearer (for example, the 180DC BITS Pilani Consulting Director, or the PEC EIC coordinator) is the co-host partner, not a BeyondTech-side owner | A one-page co-host pitch deck naming the case-league format, prize pool (A21) and date; a draft Instagram and WhatsApp creative set sized to the society's own confirmed follower base (for example, NIT Trichy's 180DC at 2,902 and ProfNITT at 2,004 followers, RE09 and RE07) |
| Phase 2: seed | 31 to 60 | Run the case league under the society's name; open founding-cohort applications at the discounted fee (A18); begin distributing a mock-drive question-bank slice specific to that campus's confirmed recruiters; track applications against T14's day-30 and day-60 thresholds | The founder (Harsh Agarwal) runs the fit interviews and mentor sourcing for the cohort; Team IdeaForge tracks applications and fill rate against T14 | The campus-specific question-bank slice (see 18.2 below); a mock-drive paper matched to that campus's own anchor-recruiter rounds; the founding-cohort offer letter stating the discount and fee |
| Phase 3: earn | 61 to 90 | Close founding-cohort enrolment; run the full fit-interview and mentoring cycle; track the cohort through to its first confirmed offers where the campus's own placement calendar allows; begin drafting the ambassador handbook ahead of the first consented result story | The founder owns mentoring delivery; Team IdeaForge owns the day-90 offers-secured checkpoint against T14 and begins ambassador-candidate shortlisting from the enrolled cohort | The ambassador handbook (built here, used only once the first offer lands, per section 17.5 and section 19); a result-story consent template |

### 18.6 What can go wrong in each phase, and the planned response

A 90-day window compresses a full acquisition cycle, from first contact with a society to a confirmed offer, into one academic quarter, which means a delay in any one phase pushes the whole calendar against that campus's own fixed, external dates (a placement season does not wait for a slow-starting case league). The table below names the most likely failure point in each phase and what the plan does about it, rather than assuming every phase runs to schedule:

| Phase | Most likely failure point | Planned response |
|---|---|---|
| Borrow (days 1 to 30) | The named society role (a President or Consulting Director) does not respond, or the role has turned over since the dossier's own research pass | Approach the role, not the individual named in the dossier, and have a second, backup society identified before outreach begins where the dossier shows more than one active non-tech society (true at BITS Pilani and NIT Tiruchirappalli; not true at PEC or COEP, where only one confirmed active non-tech society exists, which raises the cost of a non-response at those two campuses specifically) |
| Seed (days 31 to 60) | Founding-cohort applications stay below the day-30 threshold of 6 (M T14) | This is the exact trigger the stop-below threshold exists to catch; the response is to pause further case-league spend at that campus rather than continue into days 61 to 90 on an unconverting base, and to re-examine whether the co-host society's own reach (confirmed at NIT Trichy, unconfirmed at the other three picks per section 17.4's reach table) was the binding constraint |
| Earn (days 61 to 90) | A campus's own placement calendar does not produce a confirmed offer inside the 90-day window at all, which is a real risk at a campus like COEP where no confirmed placement-season start date was found [RE04] | Where a campus's own calendar genuinely cannot produce an offer inside 90 days, the day-90 checkpoint is tracked on "founding-cohort offers secured" as the model defines it (M T14), understanding that at a calendar-uncertain campus like COEP this may require the checkpoint window to flex against the campus's own confirmed internship or placement dates once they are established, rather than against a fixed calendar date this document invents |

This table is deliberately honest about the fact that two of the four picks (PEC Chandigarh and COEP) carry a thinner society bench and a less certain calendar than the other two, which is consistent with their lower Fit scores and lower stability percentages in the scorecard (M T8, T9) and is not smoothed over here to make all four picks look identically low-risk.

### 18.7 How the playbook connects to the trust ladder

The 90-day structure in this section is the operational form of the four-step trust ladder set out in section 17: days 1 to 30 execute the borrow step, days 31 to 60 execute the seed step and begin the earn step, and days 61 to 90 complete the earn step and stage the hand-over step for the season that follows. The reason this document treats the playbook and the ladder as two separate sections, rather than folding one into the other, is that a campus's own calendar (its fest dates, its internship-registration windows, its placement-season timing) decides exactly when each ladder step actually happens, while the ladder itself decides what happens and why. Reading section 17 without section 18 leaves the mechanism without a calendar; reading section 18 without section 17 leaves a calendar without a reason for its own sequence.

## 19. The ambassador flywheel: customers first, a ladder second, cash last

**Answer.** Every ambassador is recruited from the founding cohort that already enrolled and, wherever the campus's own placement calendar allows, already has a personal result to describe, never cold-recruited off a general campus post, because the one data point BeyondTech already has on cold ambassador recruitment (W13's 15 reactions against W14's 269) shows that approach does not work. The incentive ladder pays per confirmed enrolment, not a flat stipend (A14 and A15), plus a separate referral reward on the giver-and-joiner split BeyondTech already uses at DTU (A16). Payout triggers only on a confirmed, fee-paid enrolment after a cooling-off period, which is a deliberately more conservative control than most of the precedents this section benchmarks against.

### 19.1 Selection: from the cohort, not cold

The founding cohort (A17: 12 bear, 20 base, 30 bull students per new campus) is the only pool ambassadors are drawn from. A candidate is approached only after their own enrolment is confirmed and, ideally, after their own placement outcome is known, so that the ambassador role is an extension of a true personal story rather than a brand-promotion job offered to a stranger. This selection rule is the direct, deliberate correction of insight I4: the ambassador-recruitment LinkedIn post that named DTU, NSUT, Thapar and BITS Pilani drew about 15 reactions, eighteen times fewer than a Founder's Office internship post on the same page three weeks later, which drew about 269 [W13, W14]. The lesson is not that ambassadors are a bad idea; it is that an ambassador ask with no personal result behind it loses to every other unpaid-labour ask on a student's feed, while an ask made to someone who already has an offer to point to does not face that same competition.

### 19.2 Tasks

Drawing on the precedent programmes this document's own research lane verified, an ambassador's task list is kept narrow and specific, closer to Internshala's Student Partner model than to a vague "brand ambassador" title:

- Promote BeyondTech's confirmed, dated campus events (the case league, the founding-cohort's own result stories) on the ambassador's own and the host society's channels, matching the structure the Internshala Student Partner programme already uses for its own task list [RG13].
- Refer specific, named students into the next cohort's application pool, earning the referral reward (A16) on confirmed enrolment, the same structured-referral mechanism BeyondTech already runs at DTU (channel C-ORG-5, M T3).
- Run or co-run a small peer information session ahead of the next cohort's application window, using their own result as the session's proof point.
- Moderate the campus's BeyondTech WhatsApp community, the same 1:1-chat sales motion BeyondTech already relies on at DTU (W11), but now distributed across an ambassador rather than concentrated solely on the founder.

Coding Ninjas' own ambassador programme similarly combines referral-based incentives with weekly task-based rewards and a sponsorship-securing task [RG05], and Internshala's ISP similarly combines promotion, learning-session delivery and scheduled-contest participation [RG13]. The ambassador task list here is deliberately narrower than either precedent, reflecting BeyondTech's own much smaller scale at this stage.

### 19.3 The incentive ladder, in rupees

| Lever | Bear | Base | Bull | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ambassador-sourced enrolments per ambassador per season (A14) | 2 | 4 | 7 | Benchmarked against the precedent programmes in 19.4 below |
| Ambassador payout per enrolment (A15) | ₹1,500 | ₹1,000 | ₹750 | About 8 percent of the base A01 fee, compared against RiseUpp's ₹5,000-per-enrolment benchmark (F13) |
| Referral reward per enrolment, split giver and joiner (A16) | ₹1,500 | ₹1,000 | ₹500 | Policy choice, matching the DTU structured-referral channel's own reward logic |

Note that A15 is paid on an ambassador's sourced enrolment and A16 is a separate, smaller referral reward available to any enrolled student (ambassador or not) who refers a successful applicant; a single enrolment sourced by an ambassador through a direct referral could in principle trigger both, and the model treats these as the two distinct levers they are rather than collapsing them into one payout line.

### 19.5 Fraud and quality controls

| Control | Mechanism | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Payout only on confirmed enrolment | Payout triggers on fee receipt, not on application or lead submission | Matches the ladder's own design intent (A15 is explicitly "per enrolment"); prevents an ambassador from being rewarded for low-quality leads that never convert |
| Cooling-off period | A short holding window (for example, 15 to 30 days post-enrolment, a policy choice this document proposes since no precedent source specifies one) before payout releases | Guards against a referred student dropping out or seeking a refund shortly after enrolling, which would otherwise leave BeyondTech having paid an ambassador for a customer it did not actually keep |
| Per-ambassador season cap | Capped at the model's own upper bound for ambassador-sourced enrolments per season (7, the bull-case value of A14) | Prevents over-reliance on, or gaming by, a single high-volume ambassador, and keeps the founding-cohort-only selection pool from being stretched past what one season's cohort can plausibly refer |
| Fit-interview gate preserved | Every referred student still passes through BeyondTech's existing gated application, review and fit interview (W06) before enrolling | Ensures an ambassador's referral reward never rewards pushing an unqualified or unwilling student through the funnel just to trigger a payout |

No precedent source reviewed in this research specifies a cooling-off period of its own; this is a sensible design choice proposed here for BeyondTech's own ladder rather than a benchmarked figure, flagged as a policy proposal, not a verified industry standard.

### 19.7 Why the ambassador ladder costs less than it looks

At first glance, an ambassador payout of ₹750 to ₹1,500 per enrolment (A15) sitting alongside a referral reward of ₹500 to ₹1,500 per enrolment (A16) might look like BeyondTech is paying twice for the same acquisition. It is not, for two reasons that the DTU channel plan already demonstrates. First, the two levers serve different populations: A15 rewards a founding-cohort member for the structural work of running sessions, moderating a community and keeping a campus's funnel alive across a season, while A16 rewards any enrolled student, ambassador or not, for a single successful introduction. Second, both figures sit well inside what BeyondTech already treats as an acceptable cost per enrolment at DTU, as the comparison below shows:

| Channel | Cost per enrolment | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| DTU structured referral (C-ORG-5) | ₹2,000 | M T3, the base-scenario cash and founder-hour blended CAC BeyondTech already runs at DTU |
| New-campus ambassador payout (A15), base case | ₹1,000 | Section 19.3 above |
| New-campus referral reward (A16), base case | ₹1,000 | Section 19.3 above |
| New-campus ambassador payout (A15), bear case (most expensive of the three scenarios) | ₹1,500 | Section 19.3 above |

Even the bear-case, most expensive version of the new-campus ambassador ladder costs the same as DTU's own flagship organic referral channel, and the base and bull cases cost less. The new-campus ambassador ladder is, in this light, a cheaper acquisition channel than DTU's own best organic channel, not a new, riskier cost line added on top of the model.

### 19.8 The compounding case for starting small

The flywheel description in this section's own heading, customers first, a ladder second, cash last, matters because each season's ambassador cohort is drawn from the previous season's enrolled base, which means the pool of eligible ambassadors grows every season a campus stays open, without BeyondTech ever having to run a fresh, cold recruitment round of the kind that drew only 15 reactions the one time it was tried [W13]. A campus's season-1 founding cohort of 20 students (base case, A17) producing 4 ambassadors (A14's implied ratio) is a modest first flywheel turn; by the time that same campus has run two or three seasons, the ambassador pool is drawn from a cumulative base of enrolled, placed students rather than from one thin founding cohort, which is the structural reason this flywheel, once started, should need progressively less founder-hour input per new ambassador recruited, consistent with the founder-hours efficiency this document's section 20 reports for DTU itself.

**Answer.** Under the base and bull scenarios, every one of the four locked picks breaks even inside its own first season; under the bear scenario, every pick needs a second season to recover its case-league and founding-cohort costs. This is true identically across all four campuses by the model's own symmetric-treatment design, because the per-campus launch template (founding cohort size, discount, case-league cost, ambassador ladder) is held constant across picks so that differences in a campus's Fit score never quietly leak into a differently generous or stingier launch budget. Rolled up with DTU, the 12-month plan ranges from a bear-case loss of about ₹15.9 lakh to a bull-case contribution of about ₹38.2 lakh, and the founder's own time budget still fits inside his available hours even after adding four new campuses, because the hours freed by a more efficient DTU funnel outweigh the hours the four new campuses add.

### 20.3 The bear-case interpretive note on break-even

The bear scenario's season-2 break-even is not a sign the plan is weak; it is the plan working as designed under its most conservative assumptions. A 12-student founding cohort at a 50 percent discount generates only ₹48,000 of season-1 revenue against a ₹60,000 case-league cost (the bear scenario's cost is the highest of the three, since no economy of scale exists yet, per A21's own basis), producing a modest −₹12,000 season-1 cash position. Season 2's ambassador-sourced enrolments (18.0, driven by A20's multiple on the founding-cohort base) bring the campus to a cumulative positive cash position of ₹1,14,000 (−₹12,000 plus ₹1,26,000) by the end of season 2. This is the scenario the day-30, day-60 and day-90 kill-or-scale thresholds (section 18.4, M T14) exist to monitor, so that a campus tracking toward this slower bear-case path is identified and managed deliberately, rather than discovered as an unplanned shortfall partway through a season.

### 20.7 What has to be hired or delegated as the campus count grows

The 64 hours the four new campuses add, plus the ongoing fit-interview and mentoring load at DTU, are the two demands on founder time that this plan does not expect the founder himself to carry in full once the campus count passes four. Two delegation points follow directly from the roles already defined in sections 18 and 19 of this document:

- **Outreach and checkpoint tracking** (the society approach, the application funnel, the T14 threshold tracking across four simultaneous 90-day windows) is already assigned to Team IdeaForge in the launch playbook (section 18.5), not to the founder, precisely because this is the task set that scales linearly with campus count and does not require the founder's own fit-interview judgment.
- **Mentoring and fit-interview capacity** is the one task set that genuinely cannot be delegated away from people with the right background, since it depends on the same credibility (a Bain, Amazon or Google-level mentor's own standing) that makes the founding-cohort offer worth the discount in the first place. BeyondTech already has four named mentors beyond the founder (Subham Mukhopadhyay at Bain & Co., Subrat Sarangi at Amazon, Akshit Goel at Google, plus the founder himself, per the corrected mentor count, ruling X4); as the campus count grows past four, the plan's own hours math (20.6) implies this mentor bench, not the founder alone, needs to absorb a growing share of the fit-interview and mentoring load, which is a hiring and scheduling decision for BeyondTech to make once season 2 enrolment volumes (M T10) are confirmed rather than projected.

Neither of these is modelled as a line item in T10 to T12, since BeyondTech's own gross margin assumption (A24: 55 percent bear, 65 percent base, 75 percent bull, after mentor and tool cost) already treats mentor compensation as a cost against revenue rather than a separate hiring budget this document would need to size independently.

It is worth setting the four-campus roll-up (20.4) against the DTU-only scenarios from earlier in this document (M T6) to see what the new campuses actually add, rather than reading the roll-up figure in isolation. ### 20.10 What this section does not claim

This section does not claim that four new campuses will necessarily be launched in parallel, or that the founder's time budget (20.6) remains comfortable if a fifth or sixth campus is added without the delegation steps named in 20.7 actually being put in place first. The model's own founder-hours arithmetic is specific to five campuses total (DTU plus the four locked picks); a sixth campus would add a further, as yet unmodelled, slice of founder hours on top of the 196-hour total already projected, and this document does not extend the model past the four picks the brief itself asks for (B19).

The same discipline applies to the symmetric-treatment choice itself (20.1). Holding the founding-cohort size, discount, case-league cost and ambassador ladder identical across all four picks is a deliberate modelling decision, not an oversight that ignores the real differences between the picks. BITS Pilani's Fit score of 3.00 and 100 percent stability sit well above PEC Chandigarh's 2.40 and 97.5 percent, and a model that quietly gave the higher-Fit pick a larger launch budget would make the scorecard's own ranking harder to interpret, since any resulting difference in projected contribution could then come from either the ranking or the budget, with no way to tell which. Holding the launch template constant isolates the comparison to exactly what the scorecard measures, recruiter overlap, non-tech offer depth, society surface, prep vacuum, calendar fit and access, and leaves it to a later, campus-specific planning pass, once real enrolment data exists from the first season, to decide whether a higher-Fit campus in fact deserves a larger founding cohort or a smaller case-league budget than a lower-Fit one. That decision is explicitly out of scope for this document, which is built to support the selection and the launch design, not to re-open the scorecard's own weights to justify a differentiated spend before any actual season has run.
